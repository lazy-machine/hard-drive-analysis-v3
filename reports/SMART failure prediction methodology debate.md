# Peer review backs the design, not Backblaze's labels

The project's statistical design has better backing in the literature than most published disk-failure work. Discrete-time hazards fit by a classifier on person-period rows, drive-disjoint plus time-forward splits, PR-AUC alongside ROC-AUC, empirical two-parameter recalibration and a drive-level bootstrap all rest on peer-reviewed biostatistics and on Microsoft's production study, which measured 91.6% detection under random cross-validation against 36.5% under time-ordered evaluation. The weakest link is the outcome. Backblaze's own blog says it schedules a drive for replacement "once SMART 187 goes above zero", counts proactive removals as failures, and publishes no flag that separates them from reactive failures. Part of what the model learns from SMART 5/187/188/197/198 is therefore Backblaze's maintenance policy. Two clean-label fleets (Google 2007, Microsoft 2018) still found real SMART signal, so the project's SMART gain is best read as an upper bound on operator-independent predictive power, not a fabrication. The project's own last run (`build/report/report.md`, medium scale) is consistent with this reading: the SMART gain over context is +0.303 ROC-AUC at 7 days and falls to +0.168 at 365 days, the pattern both circularity and genuine degradation would produce. Several secondary choices also have documented weaknesses: Harrell's C is improper for fixed-horizon risk, a 1% per-drive-day false-alarm budget is ten times looser than Microsoft's production setting, w^0.5 was fixed rather than tuned, and decile calibration hides the tail where nearly all failures sit. Each has a cheap check, listed below.

## Scorecard of methodological choices against the evidence

## Backblaze's failure label partly restates SMART 187

The circularity is documented by Backblaze itself, which makes it the most trustworthy evidence in the whole debate despite coming from blogs. In 2014 Backblaze defined a failure as a drive "removed from a Storage Pod and replaced" because it stopped working or "has shown evidence of failing soon", named SMART 5, 187, 188, 197 and 198 as its decision attributes, and wrote that **"once SMART 187 goes above zero, we schedule the drive for replacement"** ([Beach, Backblaze 2014](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/hard-drive-smart-stats/)). The Q3 2024 report split failures into reactive (the drive won't boot or respond) and proactive (SMART stats, file-system checks), counted both as failures, and confirmed the public data carry only a 0/1 flag ([Backblaze Q3 2024](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/backblaze-drive-stats-for-q3-2024/)). The Q3 2025 report added that on SATA pods a drive sentinel cuts a drive's access once its uncorrectable-error count passes a threshold, that failure is now computed from disappearance plus Jira ticket rules, and that **a Toshiba MG08ACA16TEY showed a 16.95% quarterly failure rate from firmware-project pulls that "wasn't mechanical"** ([Backblaze Q3 2025](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/backblaze-drive-stats-for-q3-2025/)). Q1 2026 admitted that day-one failures never appear and that the quarter-end lookback leaves "a handful" of false positives ([Backblaze Q1 2026](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/backblaze-drive-stats-for-q1-2026/)).

The contrast with clean-label studies is sharp. Google's FAST '07 paper states that the SMART parameters it studied "were not in use as part of the repairs diagnostics procedure", so "there is no risk of false (forced) correlations" ([Pinheiro et al., FAST '07](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//archive/disk_failures.pdf)). Microsoft's labels came from field engineers' root-cause analysis of service incidents ([Xu et al., ATC '18](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2018/04/DiskErrorPrediction_cameraReady_final.pdf)). Both still found SMART informative, which is why circularity inflates the project's SMART gain without explaining it away. One clue points the same direction: Backblaze's 2016 count of failed drives with no warning in the five attributes was **23.3%**, against **over 36%** of Google failures with zero counts on every SMART variable ([Klein, Backblaze 2016](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/what-smart-stats-indicate-hard-drive-failures/)). A removal policy keyed on those counters would mechanically lower the silent share, although fleet age and drive generation differ too, so the gap is suggestive, not measured.

The project's own numbers fit the circularity story but cannot confirm it. In the last run the two top single attributes for 30-day failure are `smart_187_raw` (ROC-AUC 0.673) and `smart_5_raw` (0.658), both trigger attributes, and the SMART-over-context gain shrinks with horizon from +0.303 to +0.168. A policy trigger produces removals soon after a counter moves, so its effect should concentrate at short horizons; physical degradation produces the same gradient. Separating the two needs targeted checks, ordered here from cheapest to most informative. First, a **drop-the-trigger ablation**: retrain the SMART model without 5/187/188/197/198 and their deltas, keeping the other attributes the project already uses (240, 4, 12, 193, 242, normalized 3), and report its gain over context with the paired bootstrap. Whatever survives cannot be Backblaze's documented rule. Second, a **silent-subset evaluation**: restrict test failures to drives whose five counters stayed at zero through their last row, report that share for 2016–2025 (updating Backblaze's 23.3%), and measure discrimination on that subset against healthy drives. Third, a **lag analysis**: for each failure with a non-zero 187, compute the days from 187's first rise to the failure row. A spike of removals within a few days of the first rise, rather than a broad spread of lags, is the fingerprint of "schedule for replacement"; if pod type is available (Q2 2023 onward), compare SATA against SAS pods, since only SATA pods run the sentinel. Fourth, a **gap evaluation**: score each failing drive only on snapshots at least 14 or 30 days before its failure row, where a trigger-driven removal has not yet been scheduled. Fifth, a **label-burst audit**: list model-quarter failure counts in the test window, flag outliers such as the MG08ACA16TEY quarter (Q3 2025, inside the 2024–2025 test period), check for serials that report again after a `failure=1` row, and rerun the evaluation without them.

The broader representativeness critiques are weaker and older. The Register's 2014 attack rested on a 368 drive-year enterprise sample and skew from two Seagate models ([Olds, The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2014/02/17/backblaze_how_not_to_evaluate_disk_reliability/)); it concerned headline AFR comparisons, not SMART prediction. Schroeder and Gibson's 100,000-disk FAST '07 study found SCSI, FC and SATA replacement rates similar, "suggesting that operating conditions may matter more" than drive class ([Schroeder & Gibson, FAST '07](https://www.usenix.org/conference/fast-07/disk-failures-real-world-what-does-mttf-1000000-hours-mean-you)), which blunts the consumer-versus-enterprise objection. The binding limit is that Backblaze is one archival workload with one purchasing history. Lu et al. found that training on one site and testing on another made MCC "drop significantly" ([Lu et al., FAST '20](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/fast20-lu.pdf)), Alibaba's public SMART competition topped out near **F = 0.40** for 30-day prediction ([PAKDD 2020, Springer CCIS 1261](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-15-7749-9_12)), and none of the sources reviewed trains on Backblaze and tests on another operator. Backblaze's claim that more than 227 papers use the data ([Backblaze 2026](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/?p=113304)) shows benchmark status, not validity. Two data-quality issues matter for features: Seagate packs several counters into 48-bit raw values for attributes 1, 7 and 195 ([DiskTuna](https://www.disktuna.com/big-scary-raw-s-m-a-r-t-values-arent-always-bad-news/), community source), and not every vendor reports 187 or 197/198, so the 64% fill rate of `smart_187_raw` in the last run partly encodes vendor. Location fields (`datacenter`, `cluster_id`, `pod_slot_num`) exist only from 2023 and Backblaze calls `cluster_id` "not always accurate" ([Backblaze Q3 2023](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/backblaze-drive-stats-for-q3-2023/)), so environment cannot be controlled over most of the window.

## SMART signal is real at Google and EMC, but a third of failures are silent

The case for SMART carrying signal comes from large peer-reviewed fleets. Google measured 60-day failure risk after the first scan error at **39x** drives with none, after the first offline reallocation at 21x, after the first pending count at 16x and after the first reallocation at 14x ([Pinheiro et al., FAST '07](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//archive/disk_failures.pdf)). EMC's RAIDShield, on roughly one million SATA disks, found the reallocated-sector count "correlates strongly with impending failures" and reported that proactive replacement on it removes 88% of triple-disk errors; the rule shipped in a product ([Ma et al., FAST '15](https://www.usenix.org/conference/fast15/technical-sessions/presentation/ma)). Bairavasundaram et al.'s 1.53-million-drive study showed latent sector errors cluster in time and space ([SIGMETRICS '07](https://research.cs.wisc.edu/adsl/Publications/latent-sigmetrics07.html)), which supports the project's 7- and 30-day delta features: counters that start moving keep moving.

The case against SMART-only prediction is just as strong. The same Google paper found **over 56% of failed drives had no count in the four strong signals and over 36% had zero counts on every SMART variable**, and concluded that "models based on SMART parameters alone are unlikely to be useful for predicting individual drive failures" ([Pinheiro et al.](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//archive/disk_failures.pdf)). In Lu et al.'s ablation over 380,000 drives, SMART-only models averaged **F = 0.46** across five learners (best 0.58), while performance counters alone averaged 0.73 and the full SMART + performance + location CNN-LSTM reached MCC 0.95 ([Lu et al., FAST '20](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/fast20-lu.pdf)). Microsoft reported average TPR at 0.1% FPR of **27.6% with SMART only**, 30.3% with system signals and 35.8% with feature selection ([Xu et al., ATC '18](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2018/04/DiskErrorPrediction_cameraReady_final.pdf)). On Backblaze itself, Amram et al.'s interpretable trees reached about 50% sensitivity at about 10% false alarms for 30–90-day horizons ([Amram et al., MLWA 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.06509)), far below the 95–98% figures from small datasets, random splits or replacement labels such as IBM's KDD 2016 paper ([Botezatu et al.](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F2939672.2939699)).

The project's ablation follows the same design as Lu et al. and Microsoft, and its results land where they predict. Context alone is weak (ROC-AUC 0.56–0.60 at every horizon), SMART adds a large gain, and the model names a remaining life within a year for only 12.2% of snapshots from drives that do fail within a year. That last figure is the silent-failure ceiling showing up in survival form. Two interpretation details matter. SMART 9 (power-on hours) is age, so it belongs on the context side of the ablation; Lu et al. ranked 9R as the single most discriminative attribute (Youden J 0.21), ahead of 5R ([Lu et al.](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/fast20-lu.pdf)). Normalized 1, 3 and 7 carry vendor-specific semantics ([Beach, Backblaze 2014](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/hard-drive-smart-stats/)), so a per-vendor breakdown of the SMART gain would show whether pooling across vendors costs anything.

The counter-sum rule is the right kind of baseline: an operator rule, not the 3–10% detection rate of in-drive vendor thresholds that early papers beat easily ([Murray et al., JMLR 2005](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume6/murray05a/murray05a.pdf)). Murray et al. showed a rank-sum test on four attributes reaching 52.8% detection at 0.7% false alarms against an SVM's 50.6% at zero, though their healthy drives came from a lab test and failed drives from field returns, which flatters both ([Murray et al.](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume6/murray05a/murray05a.pdf)). In the project's last run the rule scores ROC-AUC 0.845 at 7 days against 0.898 for the model, but alerts on only 128 of 359 failing drives in their last 30 days against 274 for the model. Under Backblaze labels the rule nearly restates the replacement trigger, so it is the score most inflated by circularity; the model's margin over it is the cleaner quantity, and the margin on the silent subset would be cleaner still. No peer-reviewed head-to-head of a counter rule against ML on Backblaze at matched false-alarm rate was found, so this comparison would be a contribution.

## Person-period hazards are textbook; bins, censoring and the cutoff are not free

The discrete-time framing has solid footing. Suresh, Severn and Ghosh show that because the person-period likelihood is binomial, "any method that can optimize a binomial log-likelihood" estimates interval hazards, gradient boosting included ([Suresh et al., BMC Med Res Methodol 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9316420/)). Kvamme and Borgan found logistic-hazard parametrizations slightly better than PMF ones such as DeepHit ([Kvamme & Borgan, Lifetime Data Analysis 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.06724.pdf)), and Craig, Zhong and Tibshirani formalized "survival stacking" with any classifier ([Craig et al., Int J Biostat 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bpj/ijbist/v21y2025i1p37-51n1001.html)). Stacking daily snapshots per drive is a dense landmark design in van Houwelingen's sense, a pragmatic alternative to jointly modeling the SMART process ([van Houwelingen, Scand J Stat 2007](https://sugaku.net/oa/W2145997692)). Using a tree model with bin start as a feature also avoids the linear logit-hazard's inability to produce crossing hazards ([Suresh et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9316420/)).

The same sources name the weak spots. Suresh et al. note that discrete-time performance "has been shown to vary based on the number of intervals used", treat the count as a hyperparameter set at event-time quantiles, and warn that long intervals with heavy censoring bias hazards downward under half-interval conventions ([Suresh et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9316420/)). The project's last two bins are 90 and 185 days wide, and its remaining-life output is miscalibrated in exactly the coarse range: only 30.2% of drives failed by their predicted median (target 50%). Kvamme and Borgan's interpolation schemes, or a finer or quantile-based grid, are the direct remedy; a bin-sensitivity rerun with two alternative grids would settle whether this matters for the headline AUCs. The independent-binomial factorization justifies the loss, not row counts or standard errors; long-lived drives contribute up to hundreds of times more rows than short-lived ones. The drive-level split and bootstrap handle the correlation this creates, but not the extra weight long-lived drives carry in the loss; a weekly-landmark thinning run would show whether it matters.

Censoring is where the literature is most pointed. Treating censored-before-horizon units as healthy is one of the ad hoc approaches Vock et al. show loses to IPCW on calibration ([Vock et al., J Biomed Inform 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4893987)); the project's rule that post-cutoff outcomes are unknown is what they recommend. The open question is whether Backblaze's removals are non-informative. Siemroth and Park, using Backblaze in *IEEE Transactions on Cloud Computing*, report that **146,943 drives, about 31% of their sample, left without failure**, mostly through capacity upgrades, and treat them as right-censored without testing the assumption ([Siemroth & Park 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.29078)). Cohort migrations are plausibly non-informative given model and age; quiet pulls of suspicious drives would not be. The check is cheap: compare SMART counters at the last snapshot of removed-without-failure drives against contemporaneous survivors of the same model and age. Under a competing-risks reading, the project's S = Π(1 − h) is failure-free survival in a world without removals, which is the right quantity for remaining life but should be stated. A 2026 preprint adds a sharper warning: when inputs reveal calendar date, a model can learn "how much follow-up was available rather than true risk", inflating fixed-horizon AUC and Harrell's C ([Xu et al., arXiv 2607.10466](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.10466), preprint). Excluding calendar date helps, but drive model, capacity and power-on hours together date a snapshot well; a check is to train a small model to predict snapshot year from the features and, if it succeeds, confirm that test metrics hold on snapshots with full follow-up. Kvamme and Borgan also show that KM-based IPCW Brier scores are invalid when covariates predict censoring, and propose an administrative Brier score for cutoff censoring ([Kvamme & Borgan, arXiv 1912.08581](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.08581)).

Does the survival framing beat a fixed-horizon classifier? The only Backblaze comparison found, a survival tree against a window classifier, reported "similar" results ([Backblaze ML post 2021](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/using-machine-learning-to-predict-hard-drive-failures/), Blog). The project's 30-day classifier and survival model are close too (ROC-AUC 0.865 against 0.857, PR-AUC 0.118 against 0.137). The case for the hazard model therefore rests on multi-horizon consistency, correct censoring and remaining-life outputs, not on short-horizon discrimination, and the write-up should argue it on those grounds.

## Tempering has a 2007 precedent, but λ = 0.5 was picked, not tuned

Outcome-dependent subsampling with inverse-probability weights is established. King and Zeng recommend sampling all events and a fraction of non-events, then correcting by prior correction or weighting ([King & Zeng, Political Analysis 2001](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/handle/1/4125045/relogit%20rare%20events.pdf)); Dal Pozzolo et al. show undersampling preserves ranking and give the analytic probability correction ([Dal Pozzolo et al., IEEE SSCI 2015](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/7376606)). The challenge comes from efficiency and specification. Fithian and Hastie prove that standard case-control correction can be **inconsistent when the model is misspecified**, which any LightGBM approximation is, and that local case-control sampling does better ([Fithian & Hastie, Ann Stat 2014](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1306.3706)). The FAST '26 FailureMiner work found that random downsampling of healthy devices yields "unacceptable high false alarms" because it discards samples near the decision boundary ([Wang et al., FAST '26 slides](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/fast26_slides_wang-shuyang.pdf)). The project discards healthy drives at a fixed rate (2% in the last run) and should show its results are stable across two or three `healthy_fraction` values.

Tempered weights have a direct peer-reviewed ancestor. Sugiyama, Krauledat and Müller's adaptive importance-weighted ERM minimizes Σ w^λ · loss with 0 ≤ λ ≤ 1, trading consistency (λ = 1) for stability, and **chooses λ by importance-weighted cross-validation** ([Sugiyama et al., JMLR 2007](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v8/sugiyama07a.html)). Survey statistics makes the same bias-variance argument for weight trimming and warns that ad hoc cutoffs are not tuned to it ([Elliott, PMC2783643](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2783643)). Two differences matter. AIWERM assumes covariate shift with p(y|x) unchanged, while the project's weights depend on outcome and time-to-failure, so tempering deliberately biases p(y|x) and recalibration is mandatory. And λ = 0.5 was fixed. The direct fix is a λ grid (0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1) scored on validation drives with true weights by weighted log-loss and PR-AUC, together with the Kish effective sample size, n_eff = (Σw)² / Σw², for each λ to quantify the variance argument.

The clinical prediction literature argues against rebalancing. Van den Goorbergh et al. found that no imbalance correction ever raised median AUROC across 24 simulated scenarios, and at 1% events corrections pushed the median calibration intercept to −4.5 or below ([van den Goorbergh et al., JAMIA 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9382395/)). Carriero et al. found the same for calibration across six learners, but saw corrections improve the C-statistic for random forests and SVMs at 2% events while hurting XGBoost ([Carriero et al., arXiv 2404.19494](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.19494v1), preprint at retrieval). Elkan's IJCAI 2001 result is that changing class balance "has little effect" on decision-tree learners, and recommends training on the data as given and moving the threshold ([Elkan 2001](https://mlanthology.org/ijcai/2001/elkan2001ijcai-foundations)). The project's reported validation ROC-AUC rise from 0.85 to 0.89, with PR-AUC nearly doubling, contradicts the clinical prediction, but it was tied to early stopping after about 30 rounds under full weights. Byrd and Lipton show weighting effects depend on optimization dynamics ([Byrd & Lipton, ICML 2019](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v97/byrd19a.html)), so the decisive ablation is an untempered model with retuned learning rate, rounds and `min_sum_hessian_in_leaf`. If the gain disappears, tempering is an optimizer workaround and should be described as one. None of these studies tested event rates near the project's, which leaves the question genuinely open.

The recalibration step is well chosen. Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana showed boosted trees distort probabilities in a sigmoid shape that Platt scaling fits ([ICML 2005](https://mlanthology.org/icml/2005/niculescumizil2005icml-predicting)); Carriero et al. found intercept-only recalibration "did not fix slope-related miscalibration" after rebalancing, which favors the project's two parameters ([Carriero et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.19494v1)); Phelps et al. argue that trees trained on undersampled data should be calibrated by a learned map, not analytically ([Phelps et al., arXiv 2412.16209](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.16209), preprint). Kull et al.'s beta calibration beats logistic calibration across classifiers, though its main objection, that logistic maps on probabilities exclude the identity, does not apply to Platt on the raw logit ([Kull et al., AISTATS 2017](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v54/kull17a.html)). With large validation sets per bin, fitting beta and isotonic per bin and comparing validation log-loss is a cheap test of whether the miscalibration is curved; sparse late bins may need pooled or smoothed (a, b).

## Drive-and-time splits outclass the field; Harrell's C and the alarm budget lag

The split design is the project's strongest point relative to the domain. Kapoor and Narayanan's leakage taxonomy names temporal leakage and non-independence among failure modes affecting at least 294 papers, and in one corrected study the AUC gap between complex and simple models shrank from 0.14 to 0.01 ([Kapoor & Narayanan, Patterns 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10499856)). In the disk domain, Microsoft measured **TPR of 91.64% under cross-validation against 36.50% online** on the same data, explaining that a rack outage leaks "environment-specific knowledge" into both sides of a random split ([Xu et al., ATC '18](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2018/04/DiskErrorPrediction_cameraReady_final.pdf)). Lu et al.'s headline 0.95 comes from random 5-fold cross-validation ([Lu et al.](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/fast20-lu.pdf)), so published numbers are not comparable with the project's. A 2022 imbalanced-disk study warns that prior Backblaze work undersampled before splitting ([MLWA 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666827022000585)), which the project avoids.

Drift is the main caveat. StreamDFP found that 14–15 of 24 SMART attributes changed distribution year over year on large Backblaze models, monthly failed-disk shares reaching 9.7%, and drift adaptation lifting F1 by up to 53.2% ([Han et al., IEEE TC 2023](https://www.cse.cuhk.edu.hk/~pclee/www/pubs/tc22streamdfp.pdf)). The last run shows the symptoms: the alert threshold set at 1.0% false alarms on validation yields 1.78% on test, and 30-day calibration moves from 0.160% predicted against 0.128% observed in 2024 to 0.187% against 0.105% in 2025. Reporting discrimination and alert rates by test year, and stratifying the drift diagnostic by drive model so fleet turnover is separated from model decay, would make this explicit. New or rare models in 2024–2025 are a second source of decay; transfer learning work shows pooled models perform poorly on minority disk models ([Zhang et al., IEEE TPDS 2020](https://pure.bit.edu.cn/en/publications/minority-disk-failure-prediction-based-on-transfer-learning-in-la/)).

On metrics, PR-AUC next to ROC-AUC is well supported: ROC plots can be "deceptive" on imbalanced data ([Saito & Rehmsmeier, PLoS ONE 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4349800/)), and linear interpolation in PR space is wrong, so PR-AUC should be average precision ([Davis & Goadrich, ICML 2006](https://cs400-web.cs.wisc.edu/nchou/common/site/p/ml/ftp/davis.icml06.pdf)). Per-horizon AUCs match the time-dependent ROC framework ([Heagerty & Zheng, Biometrics 2005](https://edrn.cancer.gov/data-and-resources/publications/15737082-690-survival-model-predictive-accuracy-and-roc-curves)); the write-up should state that they are cumulative/dynamic and how weights enter. Harrell's C is the weakest metric. Its limit depends on the study's censoring distribution ([Uno et al., Stat Med 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3079915)), and with a fixed horizon it "can be higher for a misspecified model than for a correctly specified model" ([Blanche et al., Biostatistics 2019](https://test01.ku.dk/:obvius/pureproxy/3974268/en/publications/the-c-index-is-not-proper-for-the-evaluation-of-emtem-year-predic)). The last run shows the problem in miniature: Harrell's C ranks the 30-day classifier (0.754) above the survival model (0.731), though the survival model wins PR-AUC at 30 days. Uno's IPCW C, or demoting C to a footnote, fixes this.

Calibration checks need two additions. Decile predicted-versus-KM is valid "1-calibration" but partial ([Haider et al., JMLR 2020](https://jmlr.org/papers/v21/18-772.html)); Austin, Harrell and van Klaveren recommend smoothed curves with ICI, E50 and E90 at fixed horizons ([Austin et al., Stat Med 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7497089)), and Haider et al. add D-calibration across the whole predicted distribution. At a 7-day base rate of 0.027%, nearly all failures sit in the top decile, so finer tail bins matter more than either.

The 1% false-alarm threshold follows the Backblaze literature: StreamDFP sets daily FPR to 1% "as in" Xiao et al. 2018, and 0.3% for Alibaba production data ([Han et al.](https://www.cse.cuhk.edu.hk/~pclee/www/pubs/tc22streamdfp.pdf)). Microsoft operates at 0.1% FPR and sets false positives at three times the cost of misses because VM migration is expensive ([Xu et al.](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2018/04/DiskErrorPrediction_cameraReady_final.pdf)). Base-rate arithmetic shows why: if alert precision is measured against the 30-day base rate of 0.119%, a perfect detector at 1% false alarms tops out near 11% precision, and the project measures 3.67%. Adding the 0.1% operating point, and the share of healthy drives that ever alarm during the test window, would translate the threshold into the per-drive cost operators budget.

The drive-level cluster bootstrap is ahead of the domain, where StreamDFP-style papers report seed variance at most. A 2023 simulation found cluster bootstraps reach adequate coverage where row bootstraps do not under within-subject correlation ([Frontiers in Veterinary Science 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10728486/)), and Obuchowski's 1997 clustered-AUC variance is the analytic counterpart ([Obuchowski, Biometrics 1997, via author biosketch](https://www2.rsna.org/re/CTMW2023/CTMW%20FACULTY%20CATALOG_files/Biosketch/Obuchowski.pdf)). Resampling drives does not capture correlation across drives in one pod or on one day, Microsoft's rack-outage case. A block bootstrap over pods (from 2023) or calendar weeks would show whether the intervals are still too narrow. The last run used 200 bootstrap rounds; 1,000 or more would stabilize the tails of percentile intervals for PR-AUC with 359 failing test drives.

## Conclusion

The literature splits the project's choices into two groups. Splits, framing, recalibration and uncertainty are defensible as they stand and stricter than most disk-failure papers, so the remaining risk sits in what is being predicted, not how. Backblaze's label is partly a SMART-triggered maintenance decision, which no modeling choice can undo. Only the drop-the-trigger ablation, the silent-subset and gap evaluations, and the lag analysis can show how much of the +0.30 short-horizon gain is drive physics. Because Google and Microsoft found SMART signal on clean labels, the likely outcome is a smaller but nonzero gain from non-trigger attributes and a larger share of the rule's score attributed to policy.

The second group, tempering at a fixed λ, Harrell's C, a 1% alarm budget and decile calibration, are choices the field makes routinely and the statistics literature has already improved on. Each fix is a small change to training or evaluation: a λ grid with a retuned untempered model, Uno's C, a 0.1% operating point with per-drive alarm rates, and ICI plus tail bins. Results framed as "SMART predicts Backblaze's removal decisions, and here is the part that survives removing the trigger" would hold up to every critique in these sources. Cross-operator generalization would remain untested; no published work tests it either.
