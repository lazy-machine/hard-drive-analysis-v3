# Evaluation methodology and leakage in disk failure prediction: supporting and challenging evidence

Scope: the project splits by drive (sha256 bucket of serial, 70/10/20) and by time (train/val up to 2023-12-31, test from 2024-01-01). It reports per-horizon weighted ROC-AUC and PR-AUC (7 to 365 days), Harrell's C, decile calibration against Kaplan-Meier, an early-warning threshold set on validation drives at 1% false alarms on healthy drive-days, a drive-level cluster bootstrap for 95% CIs, a paired bootstrap for the SMART vs no-SMART gain, and calibration over calendar time. Calendar date is not a feature.

Labels used below: **in favor** = the source supports the project's choice; **against** = it challenges the choice; **mixed** = it supports one part and challenges another, or its evidence is indirect. "Non-peer-reviewed" is flagged where it applies. Every source was found or read during this session (2026-10-25). PDFs were read in full where noted.

## 1. Leakage, random row-level splits and offline evaluation in disk failure prediction

### Takeaway
The strongest primary evidence comes from Microsoft's production study (Xu et al., USENIX ATC 2018). It states outright that random cross-validation inflates disk-error prediction results and that time-ordered evaluation is required. The general leakage taxonomy of Kapoor & Narayanan (Patterns 2023) names both failure modes the project guards against: temporal leakage and non-independence. Some highly cited disk papers, including Lu et al. (FAST 2020), still evaluate with random 5-fold cross-validation, so their headline numbers are not comparable to a drive-and-time split.

### Cited Findings
- **Kapoor, S. & Narayanan, A. (2023). "Leakage and the reproducibility crisis in machine-learning-based science." *Patterns* 4, 100804 (August 2023). DOI 10.1016/j.patter.2023.100804. Peer-reviewed.** The authors survey 22 papers across 17 fields that found leakage, together affecting at least 294 papers, and give an 8-type taxonomy. L3.1 "temporal leakage" is a test set containing data older than the training data. L3.2 "non-independence" is related samples, such as repeated observations of one patient, split across train and test, and the authors suggest block cross-validation as a fix. L1.2 is pre-processing (imputation, resampling) applied before the split. In their civil-war case study, 4 of 12 reproducible papers had errors. After correction, complex models did no better than logistic regression, and in one paper the AUC gap shrank from 0.14 to 0.01. 9 of the 12 papers reported no significance tests or uncertainty estimates. In one paper, an AUC of 0.85 had a 95% CI of 0.66 to 0.95 with only 11 test events. **In favor** of the drive-level split (L3.2), the time split (L3.1), fitting the threshold and recalibration only on validation drives (L1), and bootstrap CIs. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10499856); [project site](https://reproducible.cs.princeton.edu/)
- **Xu, Y. (first author), with Lin, Q., Zhang, D., et al. (Microsoft) (2018). "Improving Service Availability of Cloud Systems by Predicting Disk Error." USENIX ATC '18, pp. 481–494. Peer-reviewed.** From the full PDF: "Existing work usually deals with prediction problem in a cross-validation manner. However, we found that it is inappropriate…" With random splits, "the training set contains parts of future data, and testing set contains parts of past data." Their example: a rack outage at time t affects every disk on the rack at once, so "environment-specific knowledge can spread to both training set and testing set due to random splitting… which causes high accuracy in cross validation but poor result when evaluating new data." They also report that features that are "time-sensitive" or "environment-sensitive" "may lead to good results in cross-validation… but perform poorly in real-world online prediction (dividing data into training and testing sets by time)." **In favor** of the time split. It also supports the decision to exclude calendar date and other time-varying context features. — [USENIX page](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc18/presentation/xu-yong); [camera-ready PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2018/04/DiskErrorPrediction_cameraReady_final.pdf)
- **Lu, S., Luo, B., Patel, T., Yao, Y., Tiwari, D., Shi, W. (2020). "Making Disk Failure Predictions SMARTer!" USENIX FAST '20, pp. 151–167. Peer-reviewed.** The study covers 380,000 HDDs over two months at 64 sites. Its headline is 0.95 F-measure and 0.95 MCC at a 10-day horizon, using SMART, performance and location features with a CNN-LSTM. From the full PDF, the evaluation method is: "We use 5-fold cross-validation… our dataset is randomly partitioned into five equal-sized sub-samples." The paper does not say whether the partition was made by disk or by sample, or whether it respected time order. The authors do report pitfall checks: the failure rate was "roughly similar over time across all 64 sites", the annualized failure rate was about 1.36%, and they call for "sufficiently long testing periods before concluding the prediction quality". They also find SMART-only models weaker than SMART+performance+location. I could not reliably align the per-group result tables in the extracted text, so S-group-only numbers are not reported here. **Mixed.** This is a leading example of random-CV evaluation that the project's design avoids, so its numbers are not comparable. It supports the project's interest in measuring SMART's marginal value. — [USENIX page](https://www.usenix.org/conference/fast20/presentation/lu); [PDF](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/fast20-lu.pdf)
- **Botezatu, M. M., Giurgiu, I., Bogojeska, J., Wiesmann, D. (2016). "Predicting Disk Replacement towards Reliable Data Centers." KDD '16, pp. 39–48. DOI 10.1145/2939672.2939699. Peer-reviewed.** The abstract reports predicting disk replacement 10 to 15 days ahead "with even 98% accuracy" on more than 30,000 disks from two manufacturers over 17 months. I did not read the full text, so I could not verify its split protocol. An IBM Research page lists the same abstract under CCGrid 2014, a venue discrepancy. **Mixed / unverified.** It is an example of accuracy-style headline reporting under extreme imbalance, which the project's PR-AUC and alert-precision reporting avoids. — [IBM Research](https://research.ibm.com/publications/predicting-disk-replacement-towards-reliable-data-centers); [KDD talk](https://videolectures.net/kdd2016_botezatu_disk_replacement/)
- **Xiao, J., Xiong, Z., Wu, S., Yi, Y., Jin, H., Hu, K. (2018). "Disk failure prediction in data centers via online learning." Proc. ACM ICPP 2018. Peer-reviewed.** I could not retrieve it directly. StreamDFP cites it as the first online disk failure predictor (Online Random Forests, labeling samples on the fly) and as the source of the 1% FPR convention (see section 5). A 2023 FITEE survey cites it for "model aging". **Mixed:** it supports time-ordered evaluation but argues for online updating rather than a single frozen model. — cited in [Han et al., StreamDFP PDF](https://www.cse.cuhk.edu.hk/~pclee/www/pubs/tc22streamdfp.pdf), ref. [37]; [FITEE survey](https://www.fitee.zjujournals.com/en/article/doi/10.1631/FITEE.2200488)
- **Han, S., Wu, J., Xu, E., He, C., Lee, P. P. C., et al. (2019). "Robust Data Preprocessing for Machine-Learning-Based Disk Failure Prediction in Cloud Production Environments" (RODMAN). arXiv:1912.09722. Non-peer-reviewed preprint; I found no published venue.** The paper studies more than 3 million Alibaba disks. It argues that public-data results (Backblaze) overstate production performance because production labels are noisy, samples are missing and failure types are mixed. It proposes failure-type filtering, spline filling and automated pre-failure backtracking. It notes that prior Backblaze studies used different train:test ratios (4:1, 3:1) and uses 10:1 by default, so headline numbers across papers are hard to compare. **Mixed:** it supports skepticism toward headline numbers. It also suggests label noise around the failure date matters, which a fixed-horizon label does not address. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.09722)
- **"Predicting severely imbalanced data disk drive failures with machine learning models." *Machine Learning with Applications* (Elsevier), 2022. Peer-reviewed. The full text returned HTTP 403, so authors and DOI were not verified.** The search snippet says prior hard-drive studies "performed undersampling on the entire data before the train–test split which might have caused data leakage". It reports Backblaze imbalance ratios in the literature ranging from 5702.72:1 to 19038.25:1, and says unbiased metrics and statistical comparisons are largely missing from the field. **In favor** of doing all resampling and weighting after the split, and of reporting uncertainty. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666827022000585)

### Inferences
- Xu et al.'s rack-outage example maps directly onto Backblaze. A random row-level split puts drive-days from the same drive, and from the same pod or vault on the same day, into both train and test. The project's drive-level hash split removes same-drive leakage. The 2024 time split removes same-day environmental leakage. Neither split alone removes both.
- Excluding calendar date matches Xu et al.'s finding that time-sensitive features look good under random CV and fail under time-ordered evaluation. Other features can still act as calendar proxies, such as power-on hours combined with drive-model cohort.
- The literature shows no agreed protocol, which makes the project's combined drive-and-time split conservative compared with the headline numbers in the disk literature (Lu 2020's random CV, Botezatu's accuracy figure).

### Gaps
- I could not access the ICPP 2018 Xiao et al. full text, so I have no numbers for it. It is cited here only through StreamDFP.
- I found no peer-reviewed paper that measures, on Backblaze data, how much a random row-level split inflates AUC compared with a drive-plus-time split. The Microsoft evidence comes from a private Azure dataset, and its random-vs-time comparison is described qualitatively.
- I did not confirm whether Lu et al.'s random partition was made at disk level or sample level.

## 2. Concept drift, model aging and transfer across drive models

### Takeaway
StreamDFP (Han et al., IEEE TC 2023) measures drift in Backblaze data directly: a large share of SMART attributes change distribution year over year, and drift adaptation raises F1 by up to roughly 27 to 53%. That supports a temporal test split and calibration-over-time monitoring. The same work, along with Xiao et al. 2018 and the transfer-learning papers, argues that a single frozen model is the wrong deployment target, which challenges the project if it is read as a deployment estimate rather than an analysis.

### Cited Findings
- **Han, S., Lee, P. P. C., Shen, Z., He, C., Liu, Y., Huang, T. "StreamDFP: A General Stream Mining Framework for Adaptive Disk Failure Prediction." *IEEE Transactions on Computers* 72(2):520–534, 2023. The conference version is "Toward Adaptive Disk Failure Prediction via Stream Mining," IEEE ICDCS 2020, pp. 628–638. Peer-reviewed. Full PDF read.** Two-sample KS tests on Backblaze models at yearly granularity: for D2, 15 of 24 SMART attributes changed distribution between 2014 and 2015, and 14 to 15 of 24 changed in each later year pair through 2018. For D3, 13 to 14 of 22 changed. For D1, 15 of 24 changed between 2014 and 2015. The monthly share of failed disks "highly oscillates", reaching 9.7% (D1) and 9.1% (D4) in some months, because disks are constantly added and retired. Drift also differs between healthy and failed disks: p(x|failed) changes more than p(x|healthy) in some models and less in others. Concept-drift adaptation improves F1 by up to 26.8%, 53.2%, 48.8%, 35.5%, 49.9%, 38.4%, 38.5% and 40.3% on D1 to D8, mostly through precision gains of 27.5 to 71.8%. For transfer learning, the authors set source-model start dates "close to those of the target disk models due to concept drift". **In favor** of a temporal test split and calendar-time calibration monitoring. **Against** treating a single frozen model's test score as the deployment number. — [PDF](https://www.cse.cuhk.edu.hk/~pclee/www/pubs/tc22streamdfp.pdf); [DBLP entry for ICDCS 2020](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/pid/207/3499.html)
- **Xu et al. (2018), USENIX ATC (above).** Their feature-selection algorithm splits the training data by time into TR1 and TR2 and drops features whose predictive power does not carry from one period to the next. They call such features "non-predictive" for online prediction. **In favor** of time-based validation and of excluding unstable time-indexed features. — [PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2018/04/DiskErrorPrediction_cameraReady_final.pdf)
- **Zhang, J., Zhou, K., Huang, P., He, X., et al. (2020). "Minority Disk Failure Prediction Based on Transfer Learning in Large Data Centers of Heterogeneous Disk Systems." *IEEE TPDS* 31(9):2155–2169. DOI 10.1109/TPDS.2020.2985346. Earlier version at ICPP 2019. Peer-reviewed.** Standard ML models perform poorly on small batches of new disk models. TLDFP (transfer learning) beats four classic ML models and two transfer-learning baselines on two real datasets. I did not confirm whether Backblaze was among them. **Mixed:** it challenges pooled models when test-period drive models are rare or new. A 2024 test split on Backblaze will contain drive models that are sparse or absent in pre-2024 training data. — [BIT Pure record](https://pure.bit.edu.cn/en/publications/minority-disk-failure-prediction-based-on-transfer-learning-in-la/); [OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1109%2FTPDS.2020.2985346)
- **FITEE survey (2023), "A disk failure prediction model for multiple issues." *Frontiers of Information Technology & Electronic Engineering*, DOI 10.1631/FITEE.2200488. Peer-reviewed.** It names model aging (citing Xiao et al. 2018), heterogeneous disks and minority samples as the open problems, and says the Backblaze data center exhibits all three. **In favor** of temporal splits and drift diagnostics. — [FITEE](https://www.fitee.zjujournals.com/en/article/doi/10.1631/FITEE.2200488)
- **"Disk failure prediction in heterogeneous environments" (IEEE, 2017; IEEE Xplore document 8046776). Peer-reviewed; authors not verified because the page did not load.** According to a search summary, it trained on 2015 Backblaze data, tested on 2016 data, and compared against Backblaze's own predictor. **In favor** of year-forward evaluation as precedent. — [IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/8046776/); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/317785956_Disk_Failure_Prediction_in_Heterogeneous_Environments)

### Inferences
- StreamDFP's finding that the failure base rate swings month to month on Backblaze matters for the calibration-over-calendar-time check. Calibration error there will mix true model drift with fleet-composition change: new cohorts entering, old ones retiring. Stratifying the drift diagnostic by drive model or age would help separate the two.
- A test window running from 2024 into later years measures the model's decay over that window as well as its accuracy. Reporting metrics by test year or quarter would show the decay that StreamDFP and Xiao et al. describe.

### Gaps
- I found no study that measures, on Backblaze data, how much a model trained up to year Y loses in AUC when tested on Y+1 compared with Y+2. StreamDFP measures distribution shift and adaptation gains but not a static-model decay curve.
- Transfer-across-model results come from papers I read only at abstract level. Numbers are missing.

## 3. Metric choice: FDR/FAR conventions vs ROC-AUC/PR-AUC; Harrell's C vs time-dependent AUC

### Takeaway
The disk literature reports detection rate (TPR/FDR) at a fixed false-positive rate, usually 1% or 0.1%, or precision/recall/F1 at such a threshold. ROC-AUC appears only as a secondary metric (Microsoft reports both). The statistics literature supports adding PR-AUC under extreme imbalance. It argues against Harrell's C as a headline when predictions target fixed horizons, and recommends time-dependent AUC, which matches the project's per-horizon AUCs. The weak point is Harrell's C under heavy administrative censoring in a short test window.

### Cited Findings
- **Saito, T. & Rehmsmeier, M. (2015). "The Precision-Recall Plot Is More Informative than the ROC Plot When Evaluating Binary Classifiers on Imbalanced Datasets." *PLoS ONE* 10(3): e0118432. DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0118432. Peer-reviewed.** On imbalanced data, ROC plots can be "deceptive with respect to conclusions about the reliability of classification performance". PR plots show the fraction of true positives among positive predictions, which reflects future performance. **In favor** of reporting PR-AUC next to ROC-AUC. — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4349800/)
- **Davis, J. & Goadrich, M. (2006). "The Relationship Between Precision-Recall and ROC Curves." ICML 2006, pp. 233–240. DOI 10.1145/1143844.1143874. Peer-reviewed.** A curve dominates in ROC space if and only if it dominates in PR space. Linear interpolation between PR points is incorrect, and optimizing ROC-AUC does not guarantee optimal PR-AUC. **Mixed:** it supports reporting both metrics, and warns that PR-AUC computed with trapezoidal interpolation is biased. The project should use average precision or the correct interpolation. — [PDF](https://cs400-web.cs.wisc.edu/nchou/common/site/p/ml/ftp/davis.icml06.pdf); [ML Anthology](https://mlanthology.org/icml/2006/davis2006icml-relationship)
- **Xu et al. (2018), Microsoft, ATC.** Following prior work, they report TPR at FPR = 0.1% "under a high precision requirement", alongside ROC-AUC. Dataset 1 AUC was 0.93 for CDEF, against 0.85 for Random Forest and 0.53 for SVM. TPR at 0.1% FPR was 36.50%, 41.09% and 29.67% on the three datasets. Averaged, TPR at 0.1% FPR was 27.6% with SMART only, 30.3% with SMART plus system signals, and 35.8% with feature selection added. **Mixed:** ROC-AUC is used, but the operational number is TPR at a much stricter FPR than the project's 1%. — [PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2018/04/DiskErrorPrediction_cameraReady_final.pdf)
- **StreamDFP (Han et al., IEEE TC 2023).** Reports precision, recall and F1 at an average daily FPR fixed at 1% for Backblaze, where daily FPR is "the fraction of falsely predicted failed disks over the total number of healthy disks in the next 30 days". For Alibaba the FPR is 0.3%. The 1% value follows Xiao et al. 2018. No ROC-AUC or PR-AUC is reported. **In favor** of the 1% per-day FPR convention for Backblaze. — [PDF](https://www.cse.cuhk.edu.hk/~pclee/www/pubs/tc22streamdfp.pdf)
- **Lu et al. (2020), FAST.** They use precision, recall, F-measure and MCC, and justify MCC for imbalanced data. **Mixed:** these are threshold-dependent metrics, not curves. — [PDF](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/fast20-lu.pdf)
- **Uno, H., Cai, T., Pencina, M. J., D'Agostino, R. B., Wei, L. J. (2011). "On the C-statistics for evaluating overall adequacy of risk prediction procedures with censored survival data." *Statistics in Medicine* 30(10):1105–1117. Peer-reviewed.** Harrell's C converges to a quantity that "depends on the study-specific censoring distribution" when follow-up differs across subjects. They propose an IPCW-based estimator free of censoring, with R implementations in survC1 and survAUC::UnoC. **Against** Harrell's C as reported. With the test window starting 2024-01-01, almost all test drives are administratively censored at the data end date, so Harrell's C depends on how long the window is. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3079915); [Harvard DBMI](https://dbmi.hms.harvard.edu/publication/c-statistics-evaluating-overall-adequacy-risk-prediction-procedures-censored-survival-data)
- **Blanche, P., Kattan, M. W., Gerds, T. A. (2019). "The c-index is not proper for the evaluation of t-year predicted risks." *Biostatistics* 20(2):347–357 (online 2018). Peer-reviewed.** With a fixed prediction horizon, the concordance index "can be higher for a misspecified model than for a correctly specified model", because it ranks event times rather than event status at the horizon. They recommend time-dependent AUC. **In favor** of per-horizon AUCs. **Against** using Harrell's C as a headline comparison, including for SMART vs no-SMART. — [KU record](https://test01.ku.dk/:obvius/pureproxy/3974268/en/publications/the-c-index-is-not-proper-for-the-evaluation-of-emtem-year-predic); restated in [arXiv 2409.05632](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.05632)
- **Heagerty, P. J. & Zheng, Y. (2005). "Survival Model Predictive Accuracy and ROC Curves." *Biometrics* 61(1):92–105. Peer-reviewed.** Defines time-specific sensitivity and specificity over risk sets ("incident/dynamic") and links them to a global concordance measure, a variant of Kendall's tau. Software: risksetROC and survivalROC. **In favor** of per-horizon AUCs. The project should state whether its horizon AUCs are cumulative/dynamic (cases: failure by t; controls: survive past t) or incident/dynamic, and how drives censored before t are handled. — [EDRN abstract](https://edrn.cancer.gov/data-and-resources/publications/15737082-690-survival-model-predictive-accuracy-and-roc-curves); [Fred Hutch software](https://research.fredhutch.org/diagnostic-biomarkers-center/en/software/time-dependent-roc.html)
- **Murray, J. F., Hughes, G. F., Kreutz-Delgado, K. (2005). "Machine Learning Methods for Predicting Failures in Hard Drives: A Multiple-Instance Application." *JMLR* 6:783–816. Peer-reviewed.** Treats drive failure as rare-event detection and designs mi-NB for "the low false-alarm regime". SVM, rank-sum and mi-NB beat the drive's built-in SMART threshold method while keeping false alarm rates low. **In favor** of reporting detection at a fixed low false-alarm rate, which is the disk convention, in addition to AUC. — [JMLR](https://jmlr.org/papers/v6/murray05a.html)

### Inferences
- The project's metric set covers both traditions: curves (ROC-AUC, PR-AUC) for researchers and detection at a fixed FAR for operators. The disk literature gives no basis for reporting ROC-AUC alone.
- The weakest item is Harrell's C. Uno et al. and Blanche et al. together suggest replacing it with Uno's C (IPCW) or demoting it to secondary, with time-dependent AUC as the primary discrimination measure, especially for the SMART vs no-SMART comparison.
- "Weighted" ROC-AUC needs a definition in the write-up. If sample weights downweight long-lived drives' many healthy drive-days, the AUC is no longer the per-drive-day or per-drive estimand found in the disk literature.

### Gaps
- I found no disk-failure paper that reports time-dependent AUC or Uno's C. Survival-style evaluation is uncommon in this literature, so the project has no direct disk-domain comparison point for these metrics.
- I did not find an explicit critique of ROC-AUC in disk-failure papers. Use of the FAR/TPR convention is implicit evidence only.

## 4. Cluster bootstrap and paired comparisons of AUCs under repeated measures

### Takeaway
Biostatistics literature supports resampling whole clusters (drives) for AUC CIs when each subject contributes many observations, because a naive row bootstrap gives intervals that are too narrow. Obuchowski (1997) is the standard nonparametric clustered-AUC reference. No disk-failure paper I found reports cluster-level uncertainty, so on this point the project is ahead of its domain.

### Cited Findings
- **Obuchowski, N. A. (1997). "Nonparametric Analysis of Clustered ROC Curve Data." *Biometrics* 53:170 (pages per the author's biosketch). Peer-reviewed. Full text not read.** Per secondary descriptions, it keeps the Mann-Whitney AUC and corrects the variance for within-cluster correlation, extending DeLong's structural components to clustered data. **In favor** of drive-level variance estimation. An analytic alternative to the bootstrap exists. — [Author biosketch listing](https://www2.rsna.org/re/CTMW2023/CTMW%20FACULTY%20CATALOG_files/Biosketch/Obuchowski.pdf); secondary description (commercial calculator site, low authority): [MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/calculator/obuchowski-clustered-roc)
- **Wu (2020). *Communications in Statistics – Theory and Methods* 49(6):1446–1461. DOI 10.1080/03610926.2018.1563176. Peer-reviewed.** Shows Obuchowski's estimator "is not efficient as it gives equal weight to all pairwise rankings within and between cluster" and proposes optimally weighted versions. **Mixed:** relevant because Backblaze drives contribute very unequal numbers of drive-days, so unweighted pooled AUCs are dominated by long-lived drives. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/lstaxx/v49y2020i6p1446-1461.html)
- **Frontiers in Veterinary Science (2023). "Nonparametric bootstrap methods for interval estimation of the area under the ROC curve with correlated diagnostic test data." DOI 10.3389/fvets.2023.1274786. Peer-reviewed.** The traditional bootstrap "resamples observations independently… without consideration of any correlation structures". The proposed cluster and hierarchical bootstraps achieved "adequate coverage probabilities which were higher than the existing traditional method when there were intra-subject correlations". pROC's default bootstrap is the traditional kind. **In favor** of the drive-level cluster bootstrap. — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10728486/)
- **Kapoor & Narayanan (2023).** 9 of 12 civil-war papers reported no uncertainty, and one headline AUC (0.85) had a CI of 0.66 to 0.95. **In favor** of the project's CIs and paired bootstrap. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10499856)
- **StreamDFP (2023).** Reports averages over five runs with min/max error bars across runs. That is variance across training seeds, not sampling uncertainty over drives. **Neutral, as a domain baseline:** it shows the field rarely reports test-set sampling uncertainty. — [PDF](https://www.cse.cuhk.edu.hk/~pclee/www/pubs/tc22streamdfp.pdf)

### Inferences
- A paired bootstrap that resamples drives once and evaluates both models (SMART and no-SMART) on the same resample is the clustered analogue of DeLong's paired test. It accounts for correlation between the two models' scores and within-drive correlation.
- With a few hundred failing drives in the test set, CIs on PR-AUC and alert precision will be wide. The cluster bootstrap reports that width honestly, whereas a row bootstrap would understate it.
- Drive-level resampling does not capture correlation across drives that share a pod, vault, batch or calendar day (Xu et al.'s rack-outage example). If failures cluster by pod or batch, drive-level CIs are still somewhat too narrow. A two-level or pod-level bootstrap would test this.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve a source that directly compares a clustered DeLong test with a paired cluster bootstrap for the difference of two correlated AUCs. The general approach is standard, but no citation was verified this session.
- No disk-failure paper with cluster-level CIs was found.

## 5. Is a 1% false-alarm rate per drive-day operationally meaningful?

### Takeaway
1% is a convention in the online disk-prediction literature: StreamDFP and Xiao et al. use 1% per day on Backblaze. Microsoft's production team works at 0.1% FPR and weights a false positive at 3 times the cost of a missed failure. Because of the base rate, a 1% per-drive-day FAR produces far more false alarms than true failures each day, so reported alert precision will be low. The convention is defensible for comparison with the literature but generous as an operating point.

### Cited Findings
- **StreamDFP (Han et al., IEEE TC 2023).** "we set the default FPR threshold as 1% as in [37] [Xiao et al. ICPP 2018]" for Backblaze HDD and SSD datasets, and 0.3% for the Alibaba production dataset. FPR is computed daily over healthy disks. **In favor** of 1% per drive-day as the Backblaze-literature convention. **Mixed** as an operational standard: the production (Alibaba) setting used a stricter 0.3%. — [PDF](https://www.cse.cuhk.edu.hk/~pclee/www/pubs/tc22streamdfp.pdf)
- **Xu et al. (2018), Microsoft Azure.** Daily imbalance is at most "3 disk in ten thousand disks could become faulty". Re-balancing raises recall but "could introduce a large number of false positives, which dramatically decrease the precision. In our scenario, the cost of false positives is high as the cost of VM migration is inneglectable and the cloud capacity may be affected." The cost ratio of a false positive to a false negative is set to 3:1 by domain experts ("we value precision more than recall"). They report TPR at FPR = 0.1%. Their FPR denominator is healthy disks, not drive-days. **Against** 1% as an operating point for a cloud operator. **In favor** of setting the threshold by cost and reporting precision. — [PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2018/04/DiskErrorPrediction_cameraReady_final.pdf)
- **Murray et al. (2005), JMLR.** The work is framed around keeping false alarms low compared with the drive's built-in SMART thresholds. **In favor** of a fixed low-FAR operating point. The specific acceptable FAR was not verified. — [JMLR](https://jmlr.org/papers/v6/murray05a.html)
- **Backblaze (Klein, A., 2016-10-06). "What SMART Stats Tell Us About Hard Drives." Backblaze blog, non-peer-reviewed, primary operator source.** 4.2% of operational drives had at least one of SMART 5, 187, 188, 197 or 198 above zero, against 76.7% of failed drives. 23.3% of failed drives showed no warning from these stats. **Mixed:** a simple "any critical SMART > 0" rule flags about 4% of healthy drives, a useful naive baseline that the 1% learned threshold should beat on recall. — [Backblaze blog](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/what-smart-stats-indicate-hard-drive-failures/)

### Inferences
- Arithmetic using Xu et al.'s base rate of at most 3 failures per 10,000 disks per day: at 1% FPR, about 100 healthy drives are flagged per 10,000 per day, against at most 3 true failures. Even at 100% recall, daily precision is capped near 3/103, about 3%. At 0.1% FPR the cap rises to about 23%. These are illustrative figures, not results. The project's measured precision should be read against this ceiling, and it is worth reporting precision at 0.1% FAR as well.
- A per-drive-day FAR differs from a per-drive FAR. A healthy drive monitored for 365 days at 1% per day gets about 3.65 alarm-days a year if alarms were independent, but alarms on the same drive are autocorrelated. Reporting the share of healthy drives that ever alarm in the test window, a per-drive FAR, would make the operational cost concrete. Backblaze's own replacement policy is per drive: replace once SMART 187 > 0.
- Measuring the share of failing drives alerted in their last 30 days is close to StreamDFP's 30-day window, so the numbers can be compared directly.

### Gaps
- I found no operator publication (Backblaze, Google, Meta, Alibaba) that states an acceptable false-alarm budget in alerts per drive per year. Microsoft's 3:1 cost ratio and 0.1% FPR are the only concrete production numbers found.
- I did not obtain Xiao et al. 2018's justification for 1%.

## 6. Reproducibility critiques and data quirks of Backblaze-based ML

### Takeaway
The biggest Backblaze-specific threat to the SMART vs no-SMART comparison is label circularity. Backblaze counts a drive as failed when it is removed, and it removes drives partly on SMART evidence, for example once SMART 187 exceeds zero. Some of the "failures" are therefore defined by the predictor. Other documented quirks are inconsistent SMART attributes across vendors, schema changes and test drives in the dataset, plus the base-rate swings that StreamDFP measured. I found no peer-reviewed paper that audits Backblaze-based ML papers as a group.

### Cited Findings
- **Backblaze (Beach, B., 2014-11-12). "Hard Drive SMART Stats." Backblaze blog, non-peer-reviewed, primary source.** "Backblaze counts a drive as failed when it is removed from a Storage Pod and replaced because it has 1) totally stopped working, or 2) because it has shown evidence of failing soon." "we use SMART statistics as evidence to remove a drive before it fails catastrophically." "once SMART 187 goes above zero, we schedule the drive for replacement." **Against** a clean causal reading of the SMART gain. Part of the measured gain may reflect Backblaze's replacement policy rather than failure physics. This is an L2-type issue (a feature that proxies the label) in Kapoor & Narayanan's taxonomy. — [Backblaze blog](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/hard-drive-smart-stats/)
- **Backblaze (Klein, 2016).** No single SMART stat appears in all failed drives. Most of the five stats are cumulative. Not all manufacturers report both 197 and 198. Drives removed for upgrades are not marked as failures. **Mixed.** — [Backblaze blog](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/what-smart-stats-indicate-hard-drive-failures/)
- **Backblaze Drive Stats Q2 2023 (blog, non-peer-reviewed).** Per a search summary, Q2 2023 introduced new fields (vault_id, pod_id, is_legacy_format) and included four "test" drives that are not in published reports. **Against** naive use of the raw panel: schema changes mid-panel and non-production drives need filtering. — [Backblaze Q2 2023](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/backblaze-drive-stats-for-q2-2023/)
- **Backblaze Drive Stats Q1 2026 (blog, non-peer-reviewed).** Per a search summary, a drive is logged as failed if it was present the previous day and absent the next. This is the operational definition behind the label. — [Backblaze Q1 2026](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/backblaze-drive-stats-for-q1-2026/)
- **Backblaze SSD Edition 2022 (blog, non-peer-reviewed).** SSDs are reported separately from HDDs. StreamDFP also evaluates SSD datasets separately (D13 to D15). If SSDs are not filtered, the panel mixes device types with different SMART semantics. — [Backblaze SSD 2022](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/ssd-edition-2022-drive-stats-review/); [StreamDFP PDF](https://www.cse.cuhk.edu.hk/~pclee/www/pubs/tc22streamdfp.pdf)
- **StreamDFP (2023).** In Backblaze data, "the SMART attributes are not all available on the first day of the collection period", and collection durations range from 21 to 81 months per model. Some models recorded only 5 SMART attributes in 2013. **Against** pooled early-year training data without handling missingness. This supports starting the training window after attribute coverage stabilizes. — [PDF](https://www.cse.cuhk.edu.hk/~pclee/www/pubs/tc22streamdfp.pdf)
- **Pinheiro, E., Weber, W.-D., Barroso, L. A. (2007). "Failure Trends in a Large Disk Drive Population." USENIX FAST '07, pp. 17–28. Peer-reviewed.** Several SMART parameters correlate strongly with failure, yet "models based on SMART parameters alone are unlikely to be useful for predicting individual drive failures." **Mixed:** it sets a prior that the SMART gain may be modest at long horizons, which supports measuring it with a paired CI rather than assuming it. — [USENIX](https://usenix.org/conference/fast-07/failure-trends-large-disk-drive-population); [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/failure-trends-in-a-large-disk-drive-population/)
- **Backblaze (2026). "How the Backblaze Drive Stats Dataset Powers Academic and AI/ML Research." Vendor blog, non-peer-reviewed, promotional.** It presents Drive Stats as a common benchmark for reproducing published results. **Neutral:** treat as context, not evidence. — [Backblaze blog](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/backblaze-drive-stats-academic-ai-ml-research/)

### Inferences
- Label circularity has a concrete consequence for the SMART vs no-SMART paired bootstrap. For drives Backblaze removes on SMART evidence, the no-SMART model is predicting a policy decision it cannot observe. The gain should be reported both overall and with SMART-triggered removals separated out, for example by whether SMART 187, 5, 197 or 198 rose just before the failure date, or by horizon. At long horizons (90 to 365 days) circularity matters less than at 7 days.
- Serial-based hashing assumes one serial per physical drive for the whole panel. The drive-level split is only leak-free if serials are never reused and model strings stay stable. I found no source documenting serial reuse or model renaming in Drive Stats, so this needs an empirical check in the data.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed reproducibility audit of Backblaze-based ML papers, and no source on duplicated rows, serial reuse, or model-name changes in Drive Stats. These need checks against the data rather than literature.
- The search summaries for the Q2 2023 and Q1 2026 blog details were not confirmed by reading the full posts.
- I did not verify a frequently quoted figure from Pinheiro et al. (about 36% of failed drives showing no SMART signals), so I omitted it.
