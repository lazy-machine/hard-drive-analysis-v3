# Survival (discrete-time hazard, GBDT) vs. binary fixed-horizon framing for HDD failure prediction

Scope note: labels are relative to the project's methodology: person-period expansion over bins [0,7),[7,14),[14,30),[30,60),[60,90),[90,180),[180,365); LightGBM logistic hazard with bin start as a feature; actuarial fractional weight for in-bin censoring; censoring at a training cutoff; many overlapping snapshots per drive. "In favor" means the source supports a specific choice, "against" means it challenges one, and "mixed" means it does some of both. Peer-review status is flagged per source. The session used about 20 tool calls. Items marked "(bibliographic, not fetched)" are well-known references whose citation details come from prior knowledge and were not re-verified in this session. Check them before quoting.

## 1. Statistical foundations of discrete-time hazard / person-period models and their use with ML classifiers (bin choice, row non-independence, actuarial half-interval)

### Takeaway
The person-period / logistic-hazard formulation is well established. Any binary classifier that optimizes a binomial log-likelihood is a valid discrete-hazard estimator, which is the basis for "survival stacking", pycox's logistic hazard, and person-period boosting. The main documented weaknesses are sensitivity to the number and placement of intervals, downward hazard bias when intervals are long and censoring is heavy, and a likelihood that factorizes as if rows were independent. That factorization is exact for the likelihood but says nothing about standard errors or effective sample size.

### Cited Findings
- **Suresh K, Severn C, Ghosh D. "Survival prediction models: an introduction to discrete-time modeling." *BMC Medical Research Methodology* 22:207 (2022). DOI 10.1186/s12874-022-01679-6.** Peer-reviewed. Label: **in favor** (person-period + ML classifiers), **mixed** (bin choice, in-bin censoring). Each subject gets one row per interval at risk. Because the discrete-time likelihood is binomial, "any method that can optimize a binomial log-likelihood" can estimate interval hazards. The paper uses logistic regression, conditional inference forests, gradient boosting, elastic net, SVM and neural networks. — [PMC9316420](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9316420/)
- Same paper, on bin choice: "The performance accuracy of discrete-time models has been shown to vary based on the number of intervals used." The number of intervals is treated as a tuning hyperparameter, with endpoints set at event-time quantiles. **Challenges** the project's fixed, hand-chosen bins unless their sensitivity has been checked. — [PMC9316420](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9316420/)
- Same paper, on in-bin censoring: it compares three ways of counting subjects censored inside an interval, one of which counts a subject only if they survive at least half the interval (the actuarial-style convention). It warns that heavy censoring or few, long intervals can bias the hazard downward and push predicted survival toward 1. **Mixed** for the project's actuarial fractional weighting. The convention is standard, but its bias grows with bin width, and the project's last bins are 90 and 185 days wide. — [PMC9316420](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9316420/)
- Same paper, on row independence: "we do not make the assumption that the event indicators within a subject are independent and have a binomial distribution." The likelihood can still be written in the form of a binomial likelihood with independent indicators (citing Singer & Willett). **In favor** of fitting person-period rows with a standard classifier loss. — [PMC9316420](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9316420/)
- Same paper: a linear logit-hazard model with a constant covariate effect across intervals cannot produce crossing hazards. This limitation of the linear logistic model does not bind a tree model that can interact covariates with the bin feature. **In favor** of GBDT with bin start as a feature over plain logistic regression. — [PMC9316420](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9316420/)
- **Kvamme H, Borgan Ø. "Continuous and discrete-time survival prediction with neural networks." *Lifetime Data Analysis* 27(4):710–736 (2021). DOI 10.1007/s10985-021-09532-6. arXiv:1910.06724.** Peer-reviewed. Label: **in favor** (hazard parametrization), **mixed** (discretization). Methods that parametrize the discrete hazard (logistic hazard) did slightly better than PMF-parametrized ones (DeepHit-style) in simulations and real data. The paper proposes two discretization grids, equidistant in time and equidistant in marginal survival probability, plus interpolation schemes, and finds that discretization matters. The PC-Hazard (piecewise-constant continuous hazard) variant was "highly competitive". — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/lifeda/v27y2021i4d10.1007_s10985-021-09532-6.html); [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.06724.pdf); [PMC8536596](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8536596)
- Logistic-hazard lineage: Gensheimer & Narasimhan (2018) parametrized discrete hazards with a neural network. Lee et al. (2018, DeepHit) parametrized the PMF. Both are implemented in pycox and wrapped in R `survivalmodels::loghaz` / `pchazard`. — [survivalmodels loghaz docs](https://rdrr.io/github/RaphaelS1/survivalmodels/man/loghaz.html); [pchazard docs](https://rdrr.io/cran/survivalmodels/man/pchazard.html)
- **Craig E, Zhong C, Tibshirani R. "Survival stacking: casting survival analysis as a classification problem." arXiv:2107.13480 (2021)**, published as **"A review of survival stacking," *International Journal of Biostatistics* 21(1):37–51 (2025)**. Peer-reviewed (2025 version). Label: **in favor**. Survival data are stacked into a large binary-outcome data frame indexed by risk sets, so random forests, gradient boosting and neural nets can be used. Stacking with logistic regression is approximately equivalent to the Cox model. The authors note the idea "is not new". Earlier version: Zhong & Tibshirani, "Survival analysis as a classification problem," arXiv:1909.11171 (2019). — [arXiv 2107.13480](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.13480v1); [IDEAS 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bpj/ijbist/v21y2025i1p37-51n1001.html); [arXiv 1909.11171](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.11171)
- **Regularisation in discrete survival models: a comparison of lasso and gradient boosting.** *South African Statistical Journal*. Peer-reviewed; authors and year not captured. Label: **mixed/against** (for inference, not prediction). In simulation, lasso-type penalization recovered the baseline hazard and covariate effects better than gradient boosting. Boosting shrank coefficients and produced more false-positive selections. — [SASJ](https://www.journals.ac.za/sasj/article/view/4948)
- **Discrete-Time Survival Analysis for Heart Failure Mortality Prediction, arXiv:2608.04140 (2026).** Preprint, not peer-reviewed. Label: **in favor** (an analogous design). Fits XGBoost with a binary logistic objective on person-period data, with the interval indicator one-hot encoded next to baseline predictors (500 rounds, depth 3, learning rate 0.02, no extra positive-class weighting). It is a close methodological analogue of the project's LightGBM setup. — [arXiv 2608.04140](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.04140)
- **Kim DI, Lai WS, Zhang KW. "Tabular Foundation Models Can Do Survival Analysis." arXiv:2601.22259 (Jan 2026, v2 Jul 2026).** Preprint. Label: **in favor**. The authors recast static and dynamic survival as a series of binary classifications over discretized times, with censored observations treated as missing labels at later time points. They prove that under standard (non-informative) censoring, minimizing the binary loss recovers the true survival probabilities as training size grows. They report better average performance than classical and deep baselines across 53 datasets. — [arXiv 2601.22259](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22259v1)
- Foundational references (bibliographic, not fetched). Label: **in favor**.
  - Allison PD, "Discrete-time methods for the analysis of event histories," *Sociological Methodology* 13:61–98 (1982), DOI [10.2307/270718](https://doi.org/10.2307/270718).
  - Singer JD, Willett JB, *Applied Longitudinal Data Analysis: Modeling Change and Event Occurrence*, Oxford University Press (2003), DOI [10.1093/acprof:oso/9780195152968.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780195152968.001.0001). Suresh et al. cite Singer & Willett for the independent-binomial factorization of the person-period likelihood ([PMC9316420](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9316420/)).
  - Tutz G, Schmid M, *Modeling Discrete Time-to-Event Data*, Springer Series in Statistics (2016), DOI [10.1007/978-3-319-28158-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-28158-2).

### Inferences
- The project's core design (logistic-hazard GBDT on person-period rows, S = prod(1-h)) matches a mainstream, peer-reviewed approach (Suresh 2022; Kvamme & Borgan 2021; Craig et al. 2025). The framing itself is well supported.
- The weakest parts are the implementation details. The literature names bin choice as a tuning parameter, and the project's bins are fixed. Bin width also interacts with the actuarial convention: [90,180) and [180,365) are long, which is where Suresh et al. warn of downward hazard bias. Median and quantile life read off a coarse step survival curve inherits the bin resolution, so a median that falls inside [180,365) is resolved only to that bin unless interpolated. Kvamme & Borgan discuss interpolation schemes for exactly this.
- The independent-binomial factorization justifies the *loss*, not any confidence statements or early-stopping splits. Validation splits must be at drive level, which the project already does with validation drives.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch Tutz & Schmid or Allison to quote their specific warnings about the actuarial half-interval assumption. Citations are bibliographic only.
- I found no source that specifically studies *LightGBM / gradient-boosted trees* for discrete hazards in a reliability (non-medical) setting.
- The SASJ boosting-vs-lasso paper's authors and year were not captured.

## 2. Is binary fixed-horizon labeling with censored rows counted as "healthy" biased?

### Takeaway
Yes. Treating censored-before-horizon units as non-events is listed in peer-reviewed work as an ad hoc approach that loses to IPCW on calibration. Dropping them instead is also biased unless censoring is independent. A separate, newer line of work warns that an administrative cutoff can leak into models through time-indexed inputs. That bears directly on the project's training-cutoff design and on its evaluation metrics.

### Cited Findings
- **Vock DM, Wolfson J, Bandyopadhyay S, Adomavicius G, Johnson PE, Vazquez-Benitez G, O'Connor PJ. "Adapting machine learning techniques to censored time-to-event health record data: a general-purpose approach using inverse probability of censoring weighting." *Journal of Biomedical Informatics* 61:119–131 (2016).** Peer-reviewed. Author list and volume/pages are from prior knowledge, not verified this session. Label: **in favor** (of not labeling censored as healthy). Common ad hoc approaches are (1) discarding observations with unknown status, (2) treating them as non-events, and (3) splitting them into an event and a non-event observation. IPCW can be added to Bayesian networks, k-NN, decision trees and GAMs, and gave better-calibrated 5-year CV risk predictions than all three ad hoc approaches. IPCW assumes censoring is independent of event time, or that it can be modeled from covariates. — [PMC4893987](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4893987); [arXiv 1404.2189 (related earlier work)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1404.2189)
- **Kvamme H, Borgan Ø. "The Brier Score under Administrative Censoring: Problems and Solutions." arXiv:1912.08581 (2019).** The arXiv page lists no journal reference; I believe it was later published in JMLR (2023), but did not verify this. Label: **against** (naive evaluation under a cutoff), **in favor** (the project's cutoff design, given a proper metric). The usual IPCW Brier score estimates the censoring distribution with Kaplan-Meier, which assumes censoring is independent of covariates. When covariates can identify censoring times, "the IPCW score is no longer valid". With administrative censoring the potential censoring time is known for everyone, so the authors propose an "administrative Brier score" that needs no censoring model. — [arXiv 1912.08581](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.08581)
- **Xu Y, Dai H, Fernandez-Granda C, Geras KJ, Shen Y. "Pitfalls of Administrative Censoring in Survival Models with Time-Indexed Inputs." arXiv:2607.10466 (Jul 2026).** Preprint, not peer-reviewed. Label: **against / warning** for the training-cutoff design. When inputs reveal their calendar date (through device, protocol or practice drift), newer records have less follow-up, and a model can learn "how much follow-up was available rather than true risk" ("administrative-cutoff leakage"). The authors distinguish this from informative censoring and from real temporal change in risk. In simulation it inflated fixed-horizon AUC and affected Harrell's C. They also show it in a real mammography cohort. Design rule: an n-year task needs "at least n years of potential follow-up after the latest input date". — [arXiv 2607.10466](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.10466)
- **Discrete-time HF mortality preprint (arXiv:2608.04140, 2026).** Label: **in favor**. Its fixed-horizon classifier baseline excludes patients censored before the horizon instead of calling them event-free, because their status at the horizon is unknown. — [arXiv 2608.04140](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.04140)
- Kim, Lai & Zhang (2026 preprint) treat censored rows as *missing labels* after censoring, not negatives, and prove consistency under standard censoring. Label: **in favor**. — [arXiv 2601.22259](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22259v1)

### Inferences
- The project's rule that failures after the cutoff are "unknown", not "healthy", is what this literature recommends. A "fails within N days" classifier that labels late-window rows healthy will underestimate risk for snapshots close to the cutoff.
- Xu et al.'s leakage mechanism applies to Backblaze if any feature correlates with calendar date: firmware-era SMART encodings, model mix, power-on-hours versus deployment cohort, or capacity. Snapshots near the cutoff have truncated follow-up. The survival likelihood handles that truncation correctly *only if* censoring is independent given x. If x encodes the date, a tree can partly learn the censoring pattern, which inflates C-index or AUC on a test set censored the same way. Mitigations: check that model, capacity and age cohort features don't proxy for calendar time, or evaluate on a forward period with full follow-up (the project's drift diagnostic may already partly cover this).
- For evaluation, Kvamme & Borgan imply that a KM-based IPCW Brier or IBS is unreliable when covariates such as drive model predict censoring (fleet migrations by model). An administrative Brier score is the cleaner option when the censoring cause is the cutoff date.

### Gaps
- I found no HDD-specific study that quantifies how much a "censored = healthy" binary classifier is miscalibrated on Backblaze. The bias is documented only in medical contexts.
- I did not verify whether the Kvamme & Borgan Brier paper was peer-reviewed (possibly JMLR 2023).

## 3. Prior work applying survival analysis / RUL to HDDs (especially Backblaze) and comparisons with classifiers

### Takeaway
Survival framings of Backblaze data exist: Cox/Weibull duration regression (IEEE TCC 2026), Kaplan-Meier life-expectancy curves (Backblaze blog), optimal survival trees (Amram et al. via Backblaze), multi-state models, and Cox alongside CNN classifiers. But the high-profile *predictive* HDD papers are mostly binary short-horizon classifiers (10-day horizons in Basak et al. and Lu et al.). Where both were tried, results were reported as similar. I found no head-to-head peer-reviewed comparison of discrete-time hazard GBDT against a fixed-horizon classifier on Backblaze.

### Cited Findings
- **Siemroth C, Park Y. "Are There Manufacturer Differences in Hard-Drive Reliability?" *IEEE Transactions on Cloud Computing* 14(2):1015–1024 (Apr–Jun 2026). DOI 10.1109/TCC.2026.3679404. arXiv:2606.29078.** Peer-reviewed. Label: **in favor** (survival framing on Backblaze), **mixed** (censoring assumptions). Uses Cox PH and Weibull regression, with exponential and Gompertz in robustness checks, controlling for age, capacity, form factor, temperature and location. HGST's failure rate is about 41% of Seagate's, WD's about 52%, and Toshiba's slightly above Seagate's. "Duration models are designed for this kind of application where outcomes for many units are right-censored." — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29078); [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.29078)
- **Ahmed J, Green RC II. "Leveraging survival analysis in cost-aware deepnet for efficient hard drive failure prediction." *Neural Computing and Applications* 37(2):1089–1104 (2024/2025). DOI 10.1007/s00521-024-10479-6.** Peer-reviewed. Label: **mixed**. On Backblaze, the main predictor is a *binary* 1D-CNN with weighted BCE (G-mean 0.692), compared against SMOTE and weighted logistic regression. Cox regression runs in parallel to rank SMART attributes, with C-index 0.958. Survival is used for explanation, not for the prediction target. — [OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1007/S00521-024-10479-6); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00521-024-10479-6)

RUL estimation is a secondary component. — [arXiv 1810.08985](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.08985); [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1810.08985)

Preprints and blog. Label: **mixed**. They predict RUL directly for failing drives and do not handle censored drives as survival data. — [arXiv 2109.05351](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.05351); [Backblaze blog on research uses](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/?p=113304)

Label: **mixed** (shows that a short-horizon *binary* classifier works well, but only with non-SMART features). On 380k drives at 64 sites over two months, ML models reached 0.95 F-measure and 0.95 MCC at a 10-day horizon by adding performance and location data to SMART.   - "Applying medical statistics to the Backblaze hard drive stats" (Hackernoon) frames removed drives as censored and applies KM. — [Hackernoon](https://sia.hackernoon.com/applying-medical-statistics-to-the-backblaze-hard-drive-stats-36227cfd5372)

### Inferences
- In the HDD literature, the binary 10-day classifier is the dominant *predictive* benchmark, while survival methods appear mainly for fleet-level reliability (KM, Cox, Weibull) or interpretation. The project's per-drive discrete-hazard predictor that outputs median and quantile remaining life is less common, and should be benchmarked against a fixed-horizon classifier on the same snapshots, at P(fail within 7, 30, 90 days), to show what it adds.
- The one direct comparison found (Amram et al.) reports similar quality for the survival tree and the window classifier. The advantage of survival framing is therefore more likely to show up in multi-horizon consistency, censoring correctness and RUL outputs than in short-horizon discrimination.

## 4. Critiques: informative censoring, competing risks, left truncation, overlapping snapshots, feasibility of RUL from SMART

### Takeaway
The biggest documented threats are (a) feasibility, since a large share of failures show no SMART precursor at all, and (b) censoring that may not be non-informative: about 31% of Backblaze drives leave without a recorded failure, mostly through capacity-upgrade migrations. The peer-reviewed Backblaze survival paper treats these removals as non-informative right censoring without testing that assumption. I found no HDD-specific literature on competing risks or on the dependence between overlapping snapshots.

### Cited Findings
- **Pinheiro E, Weber W-D, Barroso LA. "Failure Trends in a Large Disk Drive Population." USENIX FAST '07 (2007).** Peer-reviewed. Label: **against** (feasibility of individual-drive prediction from SMART). Over 56% of failed drives had no count in any of the four strongest SMART signals (scan errors, reallocations, offline reallocations, probational counts). Including all other SMART parameters except temperature, about 36% of failed drives still showed zero counts on every variable. Conclusion: "unlikely that SMART data alone can be effectively used to build models that predict failures of individual drives." Failure was defined as replacement during a repair procedure; upgrade replacements were excluded. — [USENIX HTML](https://www.usenix.org/event/fast07/tech/full_papers/pinheiro/pinheiro_html/); [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/failure-trends-in-a-large-disk-drive-population/)
- Lu et al. (FAST '20) reached high 10-day accuracy only after adding performance and location signals to SMART. Commentary notes that SMART-only warnings came too late for proactive action. Label: **against** SMART-only long-horizon RUL. — [USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/conference/fast20/presentation/lu); [DSHR blog](https://blog.dshr.org/2020/03/more-on-failures-from-fast-2020.html)
- Siemroth & Park (IEEE TCC 2026) report that "146,943 drives—about 31% of the sample—are removed without failure", attributed through personal communication to replacing old small drives with larger ones. They treat removals as right-censored, which they describe as "statistically equivalent to HDDs surviving until the most recent daily snapshot", but do not test whether removal relates to failure risk. They exclude drives with unknown installation date (21,195 drives) and judge left-censoring "not a major issue". They take Backblaze's failure determination as given. Label: **mixed**. It supports the censoring treatment as standard practice, and also documents how large the potentially informative censoring is. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.29078)
- Backblaze's own quarterly reports exclude boot drives, test drives, and models with too few units (466 drives removed in the 2023 report; Q2 2023 excluded test-only drives and models with fewer than 60 drives). These are separate from migration removals. Label: **mixed** (they are selection rules the panel inherits). — [Backblaze Drive Stats 2023](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/backblaze-drive-stats-for-2023); [Backblaze Drive Stats Q2 2023](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/backblaze-drive-stats-for-q2-2023/)
- Vock et al. (2016): IPCW, and implicitly the KM-style likelihood, depends on censoring being independent of event time, possibly conditional on covariates. Label: **against** the unqualified assumption, and points to a remedy (model censoring as a function of covariates). — [PMC4893987](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4893987)
- Xu et al. (2026 preprint) separate administrative-cutoff leakage from classical informative censoring. Both can be present in Backblaze: the cutoff plus migration waves tied to model and capacity. Label: **against / warning**. — [arXiv 2607.10466](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.10466)
- Kvamme & Borgan (Brier, 2019): when covariates identify censoring times, KM-based IPCW evaluation is invalid. Model-level migrations make drive model a strong predictor of censoring. Label: **against** naive IPCW metrics. — [arXiv 1912.08581](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.08581)

### Inferences
- **Informative censoring.** Proactive removals could be informative in either direction. Removing a whole older or smaller model cohort is probably close to non-informative *given model and age*. But if Backblaze pulls drives that look suspicious (high reallocation counts) before they "fail", those censorings carry exactly the SMART signal the model uses, and observed hazards for high-risk profiles will be biased downward. This was not verified in any source. A practical check is to compare SMART distributions of removed-without-failure drives at their last snapshot against those of contemporaneous survivors.
- **Competing risks.** Migration or retirement removal works as a competing event. Under a cause-specific reading, the project's h_k is the cause-specific failure hazard, and S = prod(1-h) is the failure-free survival *in a world without removals*, not the observed probability of failing before being removed. For RUL interpretation this is usually the quantity wanted, but it should be stated. This is inference; no HDD source found.
- **Left truncation.** Drives enter the Backblaze panel already aged. Because the project predicts *residual* time from each snapshot, conditional on being alive at that snapshot, with age as a feature, it is effectively a landmark design and conditions on survival to entry. That handles delayed entry for the conditional hazard. Left truncation would bite only if the model is used to make statements about time since installation without conditioning. Siemroth & Park instead drop drives with unknown install date ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.29078)).
- **Feasibility.** Pinheiro et al.'s result that about 36% of failures had no SMART signal at all puts a ceiling on per-drive discrimination from SMART. A survival model will assign such drives roughly the baseline hazard for their age and model, which is correct behavior, but median-remaining-life outputs for most drives will mostly reflect the population survival curve rather than a drive-specific signal. The project's 10–90% intervals should be expected to be wide.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study that tests whether Backblaze removals are informative about failure.
- I found no HDD-specific competing-risks (failure vs. retirement/migration) analysis.
- I did not fetch Schroeder & Gibson, "Disk failures in the real world," FAST '07, which documents how replacement-based failure definitions vary.
- I found no source quantifying the dependence among overlapping per-drive snapshots in HDD prediction.

## 5. Landmarking / dynamic prediction as justification or critique for many snapshots per subject

### Takeaway
Landmarking, building prediction data sets at many landmark times from the subjects still at risk and stacking them, is the established biostatistical justification for using multiple snapshots per drive. It is explicitly a pragmatic alternative to joint modeling of the marker (SMART) process. The project's daily snapshots are a dense landmark design. The known costs are within-subject correlation across stacked landmarks and implicit over-weighting of long-lived subjects.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- Landmarking supports the *point predictions* from stacked snapshots, but the stacked rows are not independent subjects. Standard landmark practice, as I recall it (not verified in this session), uses robust or sandwich variance for inference on stacked landmark data. For an ML project the analogous requirements are drive-level CV and validation splits and caution with any "n rows" claims.
- With overlapping snapshots, a drive that survives 1,000 days contributes about 1,000 times more person-period rows than a drive observed for 1 day. Failures contribute one failing row per snapshot within 365 days before failure, so each failure is replicated across up to about 365 snapshots in its final bins. Without per-drive or per-landmark weighting, this changes the effective weighting of drives. The project's "tempered training weights" may address part of this. Thinning snapshots (for example weekly landmarks) is a common alternative.
- Using *all* daily snapshots also concentrates information near failure for failing drives, which can make short-bin hazards look well discriminated, as in the 10-day classifier literature, while longer bins remain weakly identified. That matches the feasibility concern in section 4.
