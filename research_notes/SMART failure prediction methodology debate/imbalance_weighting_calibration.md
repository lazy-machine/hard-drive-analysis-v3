# Class imbalance, sampling weights and probability calibration: evidence for and against the project's choices

Project choices under review:
- (C1) Outcome-dependent subsampling of drives and snapshots, with inverse-probability (IPW / Horvitz–Thompson style) weights so that estimates stay unbiased.
- (C2) LightGBM trained on tempered weights w^0.5 instead of the full weights w. The project reports that this raised validation ROC-AUC from 0.85 to 0.89 and nearly doubled PR-AUC.
- (C3) Per-hazard-bin two-parameter Platt recalibration on the raw logit, fitted on validation drives with the true (untempered) weights.
- (C4) Calibration checked as decile mean predicted hazard vs Kaplan–Meier observed.

Labels: **in favor**, **against** or **mixed**, always relative to the specific choice named.

## Q1. Outcome-dependent (case-control) sampling with inverse-probability weights: theory, and when weighting costs efficiency

### Takeaway
Subsampling the majority class and correcting by known sampling probabilities is established practice in epidemiology, political science and statistics (C1, in favor). The same literature also shows that IPW is not the most efficient correction. When the model is correctly specified, a prior (intercept) correction or conditional-likelihood adjustment does better. Very unequal weights inflate variance, which is the problem the project ran into.

### Cited Findings
- **King, G. & Zeng, L. (2001). "Logistic Regression in Rare Events Data." *Political Analysis* 9(2):137–163. DOI 10.1093/oxfordjournals.pan.a004868.** Peer-reviewed. Logistic regression "can sharply underestimate the probability of rare events". The authors propose sampling designs that select all events plus a fraction of non-events, then correct with either *prior correction* (an intercept shift) or *weighting*, plus a small-sample bias correction. Label: **in favor of C1** (sample on the outcome, then correct). **Mixed on IPW vs prior correction.** — [Harvard DASH full text](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/handle/1/4125045/relogit%20rare%20events.pdf); [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/1E09F0F36F89DF12A823130FDF0DA462); [G. King page](https://gking.harvard.edu/publication/logistic-regression-in-rare-events-data/)
  - Not verified in fetched text (I recall it from the paper): King & Zeng say prior correction is more efficient when the model is correctly specified, and weighting is more robust under misspecification. Check §4 of the PDF before citing this claim.
  - An informal, non-peer-reviewed R-sig-mixed-models thread argues that Firth penalized likelihood is better than King–Zeng prior correction. — [R mailing list](https://r-mailing-lists.thecoatlessprofessor.com/lists/r-sig-mixed-models/threads/thread-6f131fbc7a33)
- **Fithian, W. & Hastie, T. (2014). "Local case-control sampling: Efficient subsampling in imbalanced data sets." *Annals of Statistics* 42(5). DOI 10.1214/14-AOS1220.** Peer-reviewed. Standard case-control sampling with a post-hoc correction can be **inconsistent when the model is misspecified**. Local case-control sampling (accept with probability equal to the pilot model's |residual|) stays consistent if the pilot is consistent. Under correct specification its asymptotic variance is **2× that of the full-data MLE**, even for a tiny subsample, and inflating the acceptance probabilities by c>1 cuts the factor to 1+1/c. Label: **mixed for C1**. Subsampling is defensible, but plain outcome-based subsampling is not the most efficient design, and the correction must suit a misspecified model (LightGBM approximates rather than specifies). — [arXiv 1306.3706](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1306.3706); [Project Euclid](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/annals-of-statistics/volume-42/issue-5/Local-case-control-sampling--Efficient-subsampling-in-imbalanced-data/10.1214/14-AOS1220.short); [PMC4258397](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4258397)
- Later work extends the idea. "Surprise sampling" (arXiv 2007.02633, preprint) builds on local case-control sampling. "Maximum sampled conditional likelihood for informative subsampling" (arXiv 2011.05988, preprint) argues for a conditional likelihood over IPW for efficiency. Label: **against IPW as the most efficient estimator**, mixed overall. — [arXiv 2007.02633](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.02633); [arXiv 2011.05988](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.05988)
- **Survey statistics on large IPW weights.** Inverse-probability weights remove selection bias, but very large weights increase the variance of estimates. Trimming or smoothing trades a little bias for less variance, aiming at lower MSE. — Elliott, M. R., model-based weight trimming / weight smoothing, University of Michigan ([page](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~mrelliot/trim/trim.htm)); peer-reviewed treatment in Elliott & Little / Elliott (2008) style work at [PMC2783643](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2783643), which notes that ad hoc cutoff trimming is not tuned to the bias–variance balance. Statistics Canada *Survey Methodology* paper on weight trimming: [12-001-x/2007001/9849](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/en/pub/12-001-x/2007001/article/9849-eng.pdf). Label: **in favor of the diagnosis behind C2** (extreme weights hurt variance).
- Kish's design effect, approximately 1 + CV(w)², is the standard way to gauge how much unequal weights shrink the effective sample size. Source is vendor documentation, not peer-reviewed. — [MetricGate (non-peer-reviewed)](https://metricgate.com/docs/survey-weight-trimming/)

### Inferences
- The project's weights depend on the outcome (failing vs healthy drive) and on time-to-failure (dense snapshots near failure). Weights that depend on outcome alone shift the logit intercept, which is King–Zeng prior correction. Weights that also depend on time-to-failure, which correlates with the SMART features, distort the logit as a function of x as well. That gives a principled reason why one global intercept correction would not be enough, and why something like the per-bin, two-parameter correction in C3 is needed.
- A healthy drive-day weighing ~70× a failure row implies a high CV(w) and so a low Kish effective sample size within the weighted training objective. That is consistent with the early stopping after ~30 rounds the project observed. Computing Kish n_eff for the full and tempered weights would make the argument quantitative.
- Fithian & Hastie's misspecification result is a warning for C1. If the "unbiasedness" claim rests on IPW, it holds for population-level estimands (prevalence, KM curves, weighted metrics). It does not mean the fitted LightGBM is the population-risk minimizer once tempering is applied.

### Gaps
- No fetched source gave a direct numerical comparison of IPW vs prior correction for gradient-boosted trees.
- Horvitz & Thompson (1952), *JASA* 47:663–685 (DOI 10.1080/01621459.1952.10483446), was not fetched. It is the canonical source for the HT estimator.
- Scott & Wild's work on case-control logistic regression efficiency was not retrieved.

## Q2. Effects of undersampling and reweighting on calibration

### Takeaway
There is strong peer-reviewed evidence that rebalancing harms calibration and often does not improve discrimination. That is the main case **against** training on a rebalanced or tempered sample. The same papers show the harm is mostly a predictable intercept/slope distortion. Two-parameter recalibration on held-out data with the true prevalence largely removes it, while intercept-only recalibration does not always do so. That supports C3 as the necessary companion to C2.

### Cited Findings
- **van den Goorbergh, R., van Smeden, M., Timmerman, D., Van Calster, B. (2022). "The harm of class imbalance corrections for risk prediction models: illustration and simulation using logistic regression." *JAMIA* 29(9):1525–1534. DOI 10.1093/jamia/ocac093.** Peer-reviewed. Label: **against C2** (correcting the training balance), **in favor of C3** (recalibration fixes the intercept). — [PMC9382395](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9382395/); [arXiv 2202.09101](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.09101v1)
  - Ovarian cancer case study (20% prevalence): AUROC stayed at 0.79–0.80 under every method. The calibration intercept was ~0.05 uncorrected and −1.32 (SMOTE) to −1.50 (random undersampling, RUS) after correction, which is strong overestimation. The slope was 0.99 uncorrected, falling to 0.85 under RUS. Net benefit fell, turning negative at thresholds ≥0.3.
  - Simulation (24 scenarios × 2,000 runs, event fractions 30/10/1%): no correction ever gave a higher median AUROC. At 1% events the median calibration intercept was ≤ −4.5 for corrected models vs −0.05 to 0.03 uncorrected.
  - Logistic (intercept) recalibration brought the median intercepts back to −0.07 to 0.03. The authors say correction followed by recalibration is worthwhile only if the correction improves discrimination.
  - Stated limitations: they did not test event fractions below 1%, non-logistic algorithms, or class weighting / cost-sensitive learning. The project's setting (0.004%, LightGBM, weighting) lies outside the tested range.
- **Carriero, A., Luijken, K., de Hond, A., Moons, K., van Calster, B., van Smeden, M. (2024). "The harms of class imbalance corrections for machine learning based prediction models: a simulation study."** arXiv 2404.19494. **Preprint at time of retrieval**; a UMC Utrecht repository entry lists it as a publication. — [arXiv html](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.19494v1); [UMC Utrecht](https://researchinformation.umcutrecht.nl/en/publications/the-harms-of-class-imbalance-corrections-for-machine-learning-bas/)
  - Algorithms: LR, SVM, RF, XGBoost, RUSBoost, EasyEnsemble. Event fractions 0.5, 0.2 and 0.02. Corrections: RUS, ROS, SMOTE, SMOTE-ENN. Class weighting was not evaluated.
  - "In all simulation scenarios," uncorrected models had equal or better calibration than corrected ones. At 0.02 events, only uncorrected LR/SVM/RF/XGB had intercepts near 0, and corrections caused overestimation and lower slopes.
  - Discrimination was algorithm-dependent. At 0.02, corrections **improved** the C-statistic for SVM and RF and **worsened** it for XGBoost, RUSBoost and EasyEnsemble.
  - Their recalibration was **intercept-only** (slope fixed at 1). It "did not fix slope-related miscalibration". The authors contrast it with Platt scaling, which estimates both intercept and slope.
  - Label: **against C2** in general. **In favor of C3's choice of a slope + intercept** (two-parameter) recalibration over intercept-only. **Mixed on discrimination**: their RF/SVM gains show that rebalancing can help AUC for some learners, which is consistent with the project's 0.85→0.89.
- An OHDSI 2022 symposium abstract (Yang et al., non-peer-reviewed abstract) on RF, XGBoost and lasso found that calibration "clearly deteriorated for all sampling strategies", and that after recalibration the calibration plots looked like those of the original-data models. Label: **mixed** (against rebalancing, in favor of recalibration as a fix). — [OHDSI abstract](https://www.ohdsi.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/76-Cynthia-Yang_impact-of-random-sampling_2022symposium_abstract-Cynthia-Yang.pdf)
- **Dal Pozzolo, A., Caelen, O., Johnson, R. A., Bontempi, G. (2015). "Calibrating Probability with Undersampling for Unbalanced Classification." IEEE Symposium Series on Computational Intelligence (CIDM track), Cape Town.** Peer-reviewed conference. Undersampling the majority class at rate β shifts the posterior. **Ranking is preserved**, but accuracy and calibration suffer. The analytic correction is p = β·p_s / (β·p_s − p_s + 1), and the threshold is adjusted via Bayes minimum risk. Label: **in favor of C1+C3** (undersample, then correct the probabilities). — [IEEE Xplore 7376606](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/7376606); [slides](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/calibrating-probability-with-undersampling-for-unbalanced-classification/56254737)
- **Dal Pozzolo, Caelen, Bontempi (2015). "When Is Undersampling Effective in Unbalanced Classification Tasks?" ECML-PKDD 2015.** Peer-reviewed. Whether undersampling helps depends on sample size, classifier variance, degree of imbalance and the posterior value. Label: **mixed**. — [ML Anthology](https://mlanthology.org/ecmlpkdd/2015/pozzolo2015ecmlpkdd-undersampling)
- **Phelps, N., Lizotte, D. J., Woolford, D. G. (2024, rev. 2026). "Challenges in the calibration of tree-based models for imbalanced classification."** arXiv 2412.16209. **Preprint, no peer-reviewed venue listed.** The analytic sampling-rate correction (Dal Pozzolo-type) has negative effects for random forests trained on undersampled data. Trees can be biased *toward* the minority class. Tree models trained on undersampled data "should not be calibrated analytically"; methods that learn the miscalibration (e.g., beta calibration) are more suitable. Label: **in favor of C3** (an empirically fitted recalibration on validation data instead of an analytic weight-based mapping). **Against** relying on the weights alone to restore calibration. — [arXiv 2412.16209](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.16209)
- **Elkan, C. (2001). "The Foundations of Cost-Sensitive Learning." IJCAI 2001, pp. 973–978.** Peer-reviewed. Proves that changing the proportion of negatives in training can make a cost-insensitive learner produce cost-optimal decisions. But changing the class balance "has little effect on the classifiers produced by standard Bayesian and decision tree learning methods". He recommends training on the data as given and applying cost-optimal thresholds to the estimated probabilities. Label: **against C2** (tempering as a way to change the effective class balance). **In favor of** thresholding on calibrated probabilities. — [ML Anthology](https://mlanthology.org/ijcai/2001/elkan2001ijcai-foundations)
- **Byrd, J. & Lipton, Z. C. (2019). "What is the Effect of Importance Weighting in Deep Learning?" ICML 2019, PMLR 97:872–881.** Peer-reviewed. For deep nets that can separate the training data, the effect of importance weights fades over training epochs. L2 regularization and batch norm partly restore it. Label: **mixed / context**. It shows that the effect of weights depends on the optimizer and its regularization. Here the weights changed both the optimum and when early stopping fired in LightGBM, so the AUC gain may come partly from optimization dynamics rather than the target function. — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v97/byrd19a.html); [arXiv 1812.03372](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.03372)

### Inferences
- In the clinical-ML papers, the "uncorrected" model maps to the project's **full IPW weights (w^1)**, because those reproduce population prevalence. Their "corrected" models map to **w^0 or w^0.5** (a training sample enriched in failures). So this literature predicts: (a) tempered-weight raw probabilities overestimate risk, which the project has observed, and (b) no AUC gain, which the project contradicts (0.85→0.89).
- The contradiction is plausible rather than alarming. Carriero et al. found AUC gains from rebalancing for RF/SVM at 2% events. The project's imbalance is ~500× more extreme, and its gain is tied to early stopping, a failure mode those simulations did not probe (they did limited XGBoost tuning). Still, the project should show that the gain holds against an untempered model whose learning rate, number of rounds and min_sum_hessian are retuned. If the gain disappears, C2 is an optimization workaround, not a modeling improvement.
- If C2 is kept, the literature favors C3 over leaving the probabilities as they are, and favors a two-parameter recalibration over intercept-only (Carriero). Fitting the recalibration on validation data with the true weights matches the "fit the miscalibration empirically" advice of Phelps et al.
- Elkan's and van den Goorbergh's preferred alternative is to train on population weights and move the decision threshold. That is the natural ablation to report.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study tests imbalance corrections at event rates near 0.004%, or with LightGBM class/instance weighting specifically.
- Carriero's per-algorithm numbers for XGBoost were only partly visible in the fetched HTML.

## Q3. Tempered / partial weights (weights^λ, 0<λ<1)

### Takeaway
Tempering weights has a direct, peer-reviewed precedent in the covariate-shift literature as "adaptive importance-weighted ERM" (AIWERM), with the exponent chosen by importance-weighted cross-validation. Survey statistics backs the same bias-variance trade-off through trimming and smoothing. That supports C2 in principle. The literature also says λ should be *selected* by an unbiased criterion, not fixed at 0.5. And λ<1 yields an inconsistent estimator of the population-risk minimizer, so recalibration (C3) is needed.

### Cited Findings
- **Sugiyama, M., Krauledat, M., Müller, K.-R. (2007). "Covariate Shift Adaptation by Importance Weighted Cross Validation." *JMLR* 8:985–1005.** Peer-reviewed. Verified from the full text: plain importance-weighted ERM "may not be optimal in practical finite sample cases; a slightly stabilized variant of IWERM could be practically better". The trade-off "between consistency and stability could be controlled … by weakening the weight (Adaptive IWERM; AIWERM)": minimize Σ (p_test/p_train)^λ · loss with 0 ≤ λ ≤ 1. λ=1 is consistent but high variance; λ=0 is ordinary ERM. They choose λ by IWCV, which is unbiased under covariate shift, and their example plots λ = 0, 0.5, 1. Label: **in favor of C2 (tempering)**, with the caveat that λ should be tuned by weighted validation rather than fixed. — [JMLR v8](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v8/sugiyama07a.html)
- **Shimodaira, H. (2000). "Improving predictive inference under covariate shift by weighting the log-likelihood function." *J. Statistical Planning and Inference* 90:227–244.** Peer-reviewed. The origin of importance-weighted likelihood under covariate shift. Weighting matters for the estimate only when the model is misspecified, and Shimodaira gives an AIC-type criterion for choosing the weighting. Sources attribute the flattened weights w^λ to this paper, but I did not see that in Shimodaira's own text: Sugiyama et al. cite Shimodaira for IWERM's instability, and the CHASC lecture slides describe w^α with α chosen by an AIC variant. Label: **in favor of C2**. — [Kyoto Univ. publication list](https://stat.sys.i.kyoto-u.ac.jp/titech/vita-e.html); [CHASC slides (non-peer-reviewed)](https://aiawww.cfa.harvard.edu/AstroStat/CHASC_1920/ma_20200225.pdf)
- Survey weight trimming and smoothing (Elliott, model-based trimming; Statistics Canada *Survey Methodology* 2007): capping or shrinking large weights accepts some bias for lower MSE, and data-driven methods (Bayesian model averaging over trimming levels) beat ad hoc cutoffs. Label: **in favor of the idea behind C2**, **against an ad hoc fixed exponent**. — [Elliott](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~mrelliot/trim/trim.htm); [PMC2783643](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2783643); [StatCan](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/en/pub/12-001-x/2007001/article/9849-eng.pdf)
- A 2026 preprint on handling covariate shift by model averaging (arXiv 2608.14025, **preprint**) also uses the density ratio raised to a power λ ∈ [0,1], "following (2007)". This shows the technique is still in use. — [arXiv 2608.14025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.14025)

### Inferences
- The project's w^0.5 is AIWERM with λ=0.5. Citing Sugiyama et al. (2007) gives C2 a peer-reviewed justification. Their method also suggests the obvious improvement: treat λ as a hyperparameter and pick it on validation drives with the *true* weights, using weighted log-loss, Brier or PR-AUC. That is the IWCV analogue.
- The setting differs. AIWERM is for covariate shift, where p(y|x) is unchanged. The project's weights depend on y (failing vs healthy), so the shift is closer to label/prior shift plus time-to-failure selection. Under label shift, tempering changes p(y|x) in the training objective. The raw model output is then biased by design, which is what C3 must undo. This makes C3 mandatory, not optional.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed paper that applies weight tempering specifically to outcome-dependent (case-control) weights in gradient boosting.
- I could not access Shimodaira's original text to confirm the exact λ parameterization and selection criterion.

## Q4. Platt vs isotonic vs beta calibration; boosted trees; recalibrating discrete-time hazards per interval

### Takeaway
Two-parameter Platt (logistic) recalibration is the standard low-variance choice. When it is applied to the logit, its family includes the identity map, which avoids beta calibration's main critique of Platt on probabilities. Its known weakness is that it can only fix a sigmoid-shaped distortion. With huge validation sets per bin, isotonic or beta calibration are feasible alternatives that should be compared.

### Cited Findings
- **Platt, J. (1999). "Probabilistic Outputs for Support Vector Machines and Comparisons to Regularized Likelihood Methods." In *Advances in Large Margin Classifiers*, MIT Press.** Book chapter, peer-edited. Fits a sigmoid σ(A·f + B) to classifier scores on held-out data. Label: **in favor of C3**. Not fetched in this session.
- **Niculescu-Mizil, A. & Caruana, R. (2005). "Predicting Good Probabilities with Supervised Learning." ICML 2005, pp. 625–632. DOI 10.1145/1102351.1102430.** Peer-reviewed, ICML 2005 Distinguished Student Paper. Boosted trees and stumps push probabilities away from 0/1 with a **sigmoid-shaped distortion**, which Platt scaling suits. Isotonic regression is more flexible but needs more data. After calibration, boosted trees, RF and SVMs gave the best probabilities. Label: **in favor of C3** (Platt for boosted trees). **Mixed**: isotonic may win with enough data. — [ML Anthology](https://mlanthology.org/icml/2005/niculescumizil2005icml-predicting)
- **Kull, M., Silva Filho, T., Flach, P. (2017). "Beta calibration: a well-founded and easily implemented improvement on logistic calibration for binary classifiers." AISTATS 2017, PMLR 54:623–631.** Peer-reviewed. Extended as "Beyond sigmoids", *Electronic Journal of Statistics* 11(2), 2017. Points made: isotonic overfits on small data; logistic calibration assumes normally distributed per-class scores; and the logistic family applied to *probabilities* does not contain the identity, so it can de-calibrate an already calibrated model. Beta calibration has three parameters, is as easy to fit as logistic, and was superior across classifiers (largest gains for Naive Bayes and AdaBoost). Label: **mixed on C3**. It challenges two-parameter Platt in general, but its identity critique does not apply when the input is the logit, as in the project. — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v54/kull17a.html); [EJS](https://projecteuclid.org/euclid.ejs/1513306867)
- **Guo, C., Pleiss, G., Sun, Y., Weinberger, K. Q. (2017). "On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks." ICML 2017, PMLR 70:1321–1330.** Peer-reviewed. A one-parameter temperature (slope only, on logits) was the most effective calibrator for modern deep nets. This is evidence that low-parameter logit-scale maps are often enough. Label: **in favor of C3's parsimony**. Not fetched in this session. — [PMLR v70](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v70/guo17a.html)
- Carriero et al. (2024): intercept-only recalibration did not fix slope miscalibration after rebalancing. Label: **in favor of C3's two-parameter form**. — [arXiv 2404.19494](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.19494v1)
- Phelps et al. (2024, preprint): for trees on undersampled data, learned calibration maps (beta) beat analytic corrections. Label: **in favor of empirical recalibration**, and suggests testing beta as well. — [arXiv 2412.16209](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.16209)

### Inferences
- With raw logit z as input, Platt σ(a·z + b) contains the identity (a=1, b=0). It is exactly the "logistic recalibration" (calibration intercept + slope) of the clinical prediction literature, and it fixes the intercept shift from changed prevalence plus a slope change from tempering. If the miscalibration is non-monotone-in-logit-slope (curved), Platt cannot fix it. Beta calibration or isotonic per bin would show whether it is.
- Fitting per hazard bin lets the correction vary with time-to-event, which partly answers the objection that y- and time-dependent weights distort more than the intercept (Q1). The cost is more parameters, and in sparse bins (few failures) there is a risk of overfitting and of non-monotonic jumps across bins. The usual fixes are pooling sparse bins or constraining (a, b) to vary smoothly across bins.
- Fitting on validation *drives* rather than validation rows avoids leakage from within-drive correlation. That is the right unit.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed source on per-interval Platt recalibration of discrete-time hazard models specifically. The closest analogues are time-specific recalibration in survival prediction, which I did not retrieve.
- Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana's companion paper ("Obtaining Calibrated Probabilities from Boosting", UAI 2005), which suggests log-loss boosting is closer to calibrated, was not retrieved.

## Q5. Calibration assessment in survival models: is decile mean-predicted vs Kaplan–Meier observed enough?

### Takeaway
Decile (grouped) comparison against KM is an accepted first check, the "1-calibration" / Hosmer–Lemeshow-style approach. Current methodology papers say it is not sufficient on its own. They recommend smoothed calibration curves with summary indices (ICI, E50, E90) at fixed horizons, plus distribution-level checks (D-calibration) across all horizons.

### Cited Findings
- **Austin, P. C., Harrell, F. E. Jr, van Klaveren, D. (2020). "Graphical calibration curves and the integrated calibration index (ICI) for survival models." *Statistics in Medicine* 39(21):2714–2742. DOI 10.1002/sim.8570.** Peer-reviewed. Proposes smoothed calibration curves at a fixed horizon, using hazard regression or restricted cubic splines with a Cox model, and the summary metrics ICI, E50 and E90 (mean, median and 90th percentile of |predicted − smoothed observed|). Hazard-regression curves were closer to the true calibration curve under model misspecification. Label: **against relying on deciles alone (C4)**; offers a drop-in upgrade. — [PMC7497089](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7497089); [EUR repository](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/129441)
- **Austin, P. C., Putter, H., Giardiello, D., van Klaveren, D. (2022). "Graphical calibration curves and the integrated calibration index (ICI) for competing risk models." *Diagnostic and Prognostic Research*. DOI 10.1186/s41512-021-00114-6.** Peer-reviewed. Extends the method to competing risks, which matters if drive retirement or migration competes with failure. Label: **against C4 alone** / extension. — [EUR Pure](https://pure.eur.nl/en/publications/graphical-calibration-curves-and-the-integrated-calibration-index-2/); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/2801c4e613334a56bc93ff529d45d15b)
- **Haider, H., Hoehn, B., Davis, S., Greiner, R. (2020). "Effective Ways to Build and Evaluate Individual Survival Distributions." *JMLR* 21(85):1–63.** Peer-reviewed. Reviews concordance, single-time "1-calibration" (the decile-style check), integrated Brier score and L1 loss. Introduces **D-calibration**, which tests whether the predicted survival quantiles at observed event or censoring times are uniform, so it checks calibration across the whole predicted distribution rather than at one horizon. Code is at github.com/haiderstats/ISDEvaluation. Label: **mixed on C4**. Decile checks are 1-calibration, which is valid but partial; D-calibration is recommended in addition. — [JMLR](https://jmlr.org/papers/v21/18-772.html); [arXiv 1811.11347](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1811.11347)
- A 2026 preprint, "On the calibration of survival models with competing risks" (arXiv 2602.00194, **preprint**), continues this line. — [arXiv 2602.00194](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.00194)

### Inferences
- Decile mean predicted vs KM observed is a reasonable 1-calibration check if (a) the KM estimate uses the true sampling weights, (b) it is done at several horizons, not one, and (c) uncertainty bands are shown. Adding ICI/E50/E90 at fixed horizons (Austin 2020) and a D-calibration test (Haider 2020) would answer the reviewer critique cheaply.
- Deciles hide miscalibration inside the top decile, which is where nearly all failures sit at 0.004% prevalence. Finer bins in the upper tail, or a smoothed curve on the logit scale, would be more informative.

### Gaps
- No source found evaluates calibration metrics under inverse-probability-weighted subsamples specifically, such as weighted D-calibration.

## Q6. How disk-failure prediction papers handle imbalance, and critiques of those approaches

### Takeaway
Most disk-failure papers rebalance at the data level, typically by downsampling healthy drives to about 1:10 or using SMOTE, and report classification metrics (F1, MCC, FDR/FAR) rather than calibrated probabilities. Several recent papers criticize random downsampling because it inflates false alarms. The project's IPW-plus-recalibration approach is less common and closer to the statistics literature.

### Cited Findings
- **Botezatu, M. M., Giurgiu, I., Bogojeska, J., Wiesmann, D. (2016). "Predicting Disk Replacement towards Reliable Data Centers." KDD 2016, pp. 39–48. DOI 10.1145/2939672.2939699.** Peer-reviewed. A SMART-based pipeline on more than 30,000 disks over 17 months predicts replacement 10–15 days ahead, reaching up to 98% accuracy. Cited as an example of "representative downsampling" of healthy disks. Label: **in favor of downsampling healthy disks (C1-like)**, but it does not address calibration. — [IBM Research](https://research.ibm.com/publications/predicting-disk-replacement-towards-reliable-data-centers); [videolectures](https://videolectures.net/kdd2016_botezatu_disk_replacement/)
- **Lu, S., Luo, B., Patel, T., Yao, Y., Tiwari, D., Shi, W. (2020). "Making Disk Failure Predictions SMARTer!" USENIX FAST 2020, pp. 151–167.** Peer-reviewed. ~380,000 HDDs, 64 sites. Adding performance and location data to SMART reaches 0.95 F-measure and 0.95 MCC at a 10-day horizon. I could not confirm from accessible text how they handled imbalance. Label: **context**. — [USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/conference/fast20/presentation/lu); [DSHR blog commentary (non-peer-reviewed)](https://blog.dshr.org/2020/03/more-on-failures-from-fast-2020.html)
- **Mahdisoltani, F., Stefanovici, I., Schroeder, B. (2017). "Proactive error prediction to improve storage system reliability." USENIX ATC 2017, pp. 391–402.** Peer-reviewed. Sector errors in HDDs and SSDs can be predicted ahead of time with high accuracy, even with little training data or data from a different drive model. Label: **context**. — [USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc17/program/presentation/mahdisoltani); [MSR PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/ATC17.pdf)
- **Wang, S. et al. (Samsung / Tencent) (2026). "FailureMiner: A Joint Key Decision Mining Scheme for Practical SSD Failure Prediction and Analysis." USENIX FAST 2026 (slides).** Peer-reviewed venue; slides read. Training data has "hundreds : 1" healthy:failed. Random downsampling (SC'19) and representative downsampling (KDD'16) give "more true alarms (TA), but unacceptable high false alarms (FA)" because they discard healthy samples near the decision boundary. They propose boundary-preserving downsampling: cluster the failed SSDs with K-means and keep only healthy samples inside each cluster's boundary. Label: **against naive random downsampling of healthy drives**. Supports keeping healthy data with weights rather than discarding it, though the project does discard healthy drives at rate healthy_fraction. — [FAST'26 slides](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/fast26_slides_wang-shuyang.pdf)
- "A Survey of Methods for Handling Disk Data Imbalance" (arXiv 2310.08867, 2023, **preprint**). Most disk-failure work that tackles imbalance uses data-level methods. It sorts methods into data-level, algorithm-level and hybrid. Label: **context**. — [arXiv 2310.08867](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.08867)
- A 2025 Backblaze/Google study (arXiv 2506.13838, **preprint**) reports ~7M samples with ~0.05% failures and downsamples to a 1:10 failed:healthy ratio "similar to previous work". Label: **context** (shows the common practice). — [arXiv 2506.13838](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.13838)
- Cost-sensitive learning for disk failure (CSLE, DATE 2022) treats false alarms (migration overhead) and missed failures as different costs, and reports a 52–96% reduction in misclassification cost vs a rank model. Label: **in favor of a cost/threshold-based approach** (Elkan-style) over rebalancing. — [DATE 2022 PDF](https://past.date-conference.com/proceedings-archive/2022/pdf/0878.pdf)
- A Springer *Neural Computing and Applications* paper (2024) compares 1D-CNNs with SMOTE and weighted logistic regression on Backblaze and reports the CNNs did better. Label: **context**. — [Springer s00521-024-10479-6](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00521-024-10479-6)
- RODMAN (arXiv 1912.09722, **preprint**) uses time-ordered train/test splits with sliding windows, and notes that Backblaze studies used varying train:test ratios (4:1, 3:1). Label: **context on evaluation design**. — [arXiv 1912.09722](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.09722)

### Inferences
- The disk-failure literature rarely reports calibration. The project's focus on calibrated hazards (C3, C4) is a contribution relative to that literature, and comparisons to published F1/MCC figures need care, since those figures often come from downsampled test sets with inflated prevalence.
- The FAST'26 critique of downsampling targets *classification boundaries* learned from discarded data. Because the project discards healthy drives (healthy_fraction) rather than only reweighting them, it is partly exposed to the same critique. Reporting results across several healthy_fraction values would answer it.
